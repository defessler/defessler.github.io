wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial basics

The Main Window

Profiles live in the left sidebar. The selected profile's windows fill the right pane. Every row shows the app, its title at save time, and the "Where & How" it restores: monitor, virtual desktop, size, position, and state.

The Coding profile: six windows across two monitors and two virtual desktops.

  • ⌨ chip - The profile has a global hotkey.
  • ● / ○ dot - Bound / not bound to a monitor setup (see auto-activation).
  • Footer hints - The whole app drives from the keyboard: j/k to move, Enter to load, / to filter, ? for the full map.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial profiles

Saving & Loading Layouts

Arrange your windows the way you like, hit "+ new profile", name it, done. WLM captures every visible window's position, size, state (normal / minimized / maximized), monitor, and virtual desktop.

  • Load - Puts every captured window back, even after apps restarted, titles changed, or Windows shoved everything onto one screen. The matcher tries to re-find windows by process, class, title signature, owner identity, and position.
  • Update - Re-captures the current arrangement into the selected profile (your hotkey, binding, and history are preserved).
  • Load details - Shows a per-window report of the last load: what matched, what moved, what was missing.
  • Apps that open their windows late (IDEs re-floating tool panels, Electron apps) are re-applied automatically for up to ~2 minutes after a load.
Profiles are plain JSON in %APPDATA%\window-layout-manager\profiles (the "FOLDER" button opens it). They're hand-editable, with schema autocomplete in most editors.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial hotkeys

Global Hotkeys

Click the hotkey chip on any profile to bind a system-wide shortcut. Press the combination you want. WLM records it live and warns about conflicts with other profiles or the auto-activation hotkey.

Hotkeys fire from anywhere. The WLM window doesn't need to be open.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial display

Display Zoom, Resolution & Arrangement

A profile can set your display scaling - the percentage in Settings → System → Display that everyone calls zoom - and your resolution, before it puts your windows back. Click the Display chip on any profile to set it up.

Every dropdown is filled from what Windows actually reports for that monitor. Nothing is hardcoded.

Displays are named by where they are and what size they run - "Left 4K", "Right 1080p" - because Windows reports the same useless name, "Generic PnP Monitor", for nearly every monitor. A virtual display says so.

  • Scaling - Pick a percentage, or pick Step up one / Step down one. The step options are relative: they move one rung along whatever ladder that monitor supports, worked out at the moment you press the key. That's the one you want on a hotkey.
  • Resolution - Every mode the driver offers, with its refresh rate. Leave either dropdown on "Leave unchanged" and the profile won't touch it.
  • Each row shows what the monitor is set to right now, so you can see what you're changing from.

Arranging your monitors

The picture at the top of the editor isn't just a picker. Drag a monitor and it moves, the same way the numbered boxes move in Windows' own Display Settings - and a profile can remember where you put them.

A monitor clicks onto its neighbour's edge when it comes close, gets pushed back out if you drop it on top of something, and gets pulled back into contact if you drop it out in empty space. A short drag still just selects, so picking a display to change its resolution works the way it always did. Without a mouse: select a display, hold Shift and press an arrow key to send it to that side of its nearest neighbour, flush against it.

Windows quietly enforces three things about any arrangement, and will rewrite yours if you break them - which is how monitors end up somewhere you never put them. WLM settles the layout before writing it instead: the primary display sits at the origin, nothing overlaps, and every display touches another one. If closing a gap means moving a display you weren't touching, it tells you which one.

Saving the profile saves the arrangement with it, and loading the profile puts the monitors back before it restores any windows. Positions are stored for every display rather than only the ones you moved: "the portrait one is on the left" means nothing without knowing where the other one is, so half an arrangement would restore a different desk on any day the rest had drifted.

Apply now tries the whole thing on your real displays with the usual 15-second Keep-or-Revert countdown - and the revert puts the arrangement back too, not just the resolutions. Rearranging is the one change here that survives a reboot, so it's the one that most needs an undo that still works when you can't read the screen.

Under the picker, a chip says which of three things is true of the arrangement, and the button beside it does the matching thing:

  • Not saved with this profile → Save this arrangement. Pins your monitors exactly where they sit right now. Worth knowing: this works when you haven't dragged anything. If your desk is already the way you want it, that's the layout to capture, and there's nothing to move to say so.
  • Changed from your current arrangement → Revert. Puts the boxes back where your monitors physically are. It leaves a saved arrangement alone.
  • Saved with this profile → Remove arrangement. Drops the arrangement from the profile without touching the rest of the display step.

Changing several monitors together

The picker takes more than one monitor at a time. Ctrl+click adds or removes one, Shift+click takes the run between two, the same gesture the visual editor uses for windows. Every selected monitor still gets its own card, so nothing is hidden behind the group, and a strip above them applies one setting to all of them at once.

Scaling there is step up or step down rather than a percentage. Every display has its own ladder of supported scales, and 150% is not necessarily a rung on all of them, so a step moves one rung along whatever ladder each display actually has.

Earlier arrangements

The Keep-or-Revert countdown covers the change you just made. It doesn't cover the one from yesterday. History in the display editor does.

WLM records your arrangement before every change, whatever caused it: a profile load, a hotkey, Apply now, even a restore from this list. Each row says what caused it and how long ago it was.

Preview draws that arrangement in the picker at the top of the editor. Nothing is applied. Nothing on your desk moves. You can click down the list comparing them as fast as you like, because drawing costs nothing. Restore this one is the separate step that actually writes it, and it goes through the same 15-second countdown as everything else.

The list belongs to the monitors it describes rather than to a profile, so a layout recorded at your desk is never offered to you on a different set of displays. A row naming a monitor you no longer have is greyed out and says which one is missing, instead of vanishing and leaving you wondering where your history went.

Your windows come with the monitors

Every window lives in one coordinate space that spans all your displays. Move a monitor and that space shifts underneath, so a window that doesn't move ends up on a different screen, or off the desktop entirely. Windows itself only rescues the ones that land completely off-screen.

Swap your left and right monitors now and the windows come with them, keeping the same spot on the same panel. Minimised and maximised windows are left where they are: a maximised window already follows its monitor, and a minimised one has no position worth keeping.

Zoom to resolution rules

These live in Settings → Auto-activation → Zoom to resolution rules, not in a profile, because they describe your hardware rather than a layout. If 150% means 2560×1440 on your 4K panel, that's true no matter which profile you're loading.

Pair a percentage with a resolution for a display, and from then on whenever that display gets set to that zoom level the resolution comes with it: by a profile, by a step hotkey, by anything.

Every rule names its display and says how it finds it.

Each rule shows what it's matched by. Rules are filed against the part of a display's hardware identity that doesn't change between sessions, so one set for a Parsec virtual display still applies the next time you connect. Two displays of the same model stay separate, because the key includes which output each one is on.

Picking a resolution

Resolution is two dropdowns, the way Windows and NVIDIA both do it: one for size, one for refresh rate. Sizes are grouped by shape (16:9, 4:3, 21:9) and the largest one the driver offers is marked. Every size it reports is listed, however small.

Some displays offer a lot of them. One on the test machine reports 227 modes, so the resolution list has a search box at the top of it. Open the list and the box is the first thing in it, above the sizes, with the shape headings staying put as you narrow things down. It goes on the numbers, because nobody agrees on the separator and the list itself uses a × that isn't on your keyboard. Type 1440 for every mode with 1440 on either side, 2560 1440 or 2560x1440 for that one, or 25 14 and get it just as well. A word rather than a number searches the labels. Arrow keys, Home, End, Enter and Esc all do what they should, and a query that matches nothing says so rather than leaving a row that has nothing to do with what you typed.

Pick a size that can't do your current refresh rate and WLM keeps the rate where it can, otherwise falls back to the highest that size offers and tells you it did. It won't silently jump to whatever was first in the list.

Landscape or portrait

The orientation dropdown sits before the resolution, because it decides what resolutions there are. Landscape, portrait, and both of them flipped, the same four Windows offers.

Windows only ever reports the resolutions for the orientation a display is currently in. A rotated monitor reports 1440×2560, 1440×1920 and so on, with no landscape sizes at all. So WLM works out the list for the orientation you pick rather than asking for one that doesn't exist, and flipping between landscape and portrait keeps the same physical resolution selected instead of resetting you to the top.

The sizes are written long side first whichever way the panel is facing, so a rotated monitor reads 2560×1440 rather than backwards. Only the label is normalised. What gets sent to Windows stays in the orientation Windows is speaking. A display that genuinely offers both shapes keeps the true order, since flattening it there would make 1920×1080 and 1080×1920 read identically.

Resolutions are stored the way you'll see them: pick portrait 1080×1920 and that is what's saved, which is also what Windows reports back once it's applied.

Resolutions your display doesn't list

A mode your display doesn't currently offer is still saved. It's flagged as not offered yet and applies the moment the driver starts listing it. That matters for virtual displays, whose mode lists change depending on what the driver has been told about. A saved resolution your current display can't do shows up filled in and flagged, rather than as an empty box that looks like you never set anything.

Adding resolutions to a virtual display

A virtual display only offers what its driver was built to know about, and WLM names the driver behind yours: Parsec, Apollo, spacedesk, the community Virtual Display Driver, a Remote Desktop session, a virtual machine's adapter. A display from a driver it doesn't recognise still reads as virtual rather than being mistaken for a monitor on your desk.

The Add resolutions button appears when a virtual display is connected, or when a driver WLM can write to is installed with its display down. That second case is the useful one: it's how you register a size before the next session rather than after.

Parsec is the only driver WLM adds resolutions to itself, because it's the only one that publishes a way for another app to do it. Two custom resolutions, behind one administrator prompt. Disconnect and reconnect the session and they show up in Windows display settings and in WLM's dropdowns.

For every other driver the window says who can change it instead. spacedesk takes its resolution from the client device, Apollo builds its display to match each stream, the community driver reads a settings file of its own. A button that quietly did nothing would be worse than being told where to go.

Slots 2 to 4 are shown read-only — Parsec rewrites those itself on every connect.

Two limits worth knowing, because the obvious expectation is wrong:

  • They won't appear in Parsec's own resolution picker. That list is a fixed set built into Parsec, not read from your display. Apply the resolution from WLM instead, or set Parsec's host Resolution to "Keep Host Resolution" so it stops overriding what WLM sets.
  • Parsec owns three of the five slots. It rewrites slots 2, 3 and 4 on every connect with your client's screen sizes, so WLM only ever touches slots 0 and 1.

Zoom presets

Tick Display-only preset and the profile stops being a window layout and becomes purely a display preset. No windows saved, no windows moved - it just applies the scaling and resolution.

"Desk zoom" is a display-only preset on Ctrl+Alt+0. It has no windows - just a display change.

Because it's still a profile, it picks up everything profiles already have: a hotkey through the same capture dialog, a spot in the tray menu, the Jump List, the command palette, --load=, and wlm:// links. Display-only presets are kept out of auto-activation, so binding one to a monitor setup can't make it beat the layout profile you actually wanted.

Turning it on, and what it can't promise

There's a kill switch under Settings → Auto-activation → Display configuration. It's on by default. Turn it off and a profile's saved scaling and resolution are skipped on load — and WLM tells you that's why, rather than doing nothing quietly.

Changing resolution uses a documented, stable Windows API. Changing the scaling percentage does not - Windows exposes no supported way to do it, so WLM uses the same internal call the Settings app does. Microsoft could change it in any update, so it sits behind a capability check: if your build stops accepting it, the scaling controls switch off and tell you why, and resolution carries on working.

The same honesty runs through the rest. Ask for a percentage your monitor can't do and you're told it was clamped and to what. Ask for a resolution your driver doesn't offer and you're told that too. After every change WLM re-reads the display and checks the value actually landed - there's a known Windows 11 24H2 bug where a resolution change against a virtual display reports success while nothing happens, and this is what catches it.

Some virtual display adapters, Parsec's among them, report a scaling range that disagrees with what Windows is really doing. Where that happens the editor shows you both numbers and says the control may not behave, rather than printing a figure it knows is contradicted.

The Visual Editor

Switch the view to "VISUAL" for a live minimap of your saved layout: monitors to scale, one tab per virtual desktop, every window draggable.

Drag to move, corner-drag to resize. Edges snap to monitors and other windows (Alt disables snapping).

  • Select a window to get "Edit", "Block app", and "Remove" in the panel below the canvas.
  • Select several at once the way you select files: Ctrl+click adds or removes one, Shift+click takes everything between what you clicked and where you started, and Ctrl+Shift+click adds a whole run to what you already had. Shift measures from where you started rather than from the last thing you touched, so a second Shift+click shrinks the same range instead of walking it along.
  • With more than one selected, the panel below offers what makes sense for a group: move to monitor, set state, and remove. Position and size are deliberately not there. One X applied to twelve windows stacks all twelve in the same place.
  • Drag a window between monitors. Its target monitor and relative position will update automatically.
  • Every window can be given its own colour from the edit dialog, so the ones you care about stand out on the canvas.
  • Edits auto-save after a beat. The "unsaved" badge tells you when a write is pending.

Reaching a window behind another

A window sitting on top of another hides it. The "+N" badge tells you something is under there and clicking cycles through the stack, but that doesn't help when you want to drag the one underneath. Each window has a collapse control that folds it down to its title row, and "Collapse overlapping" in the legend folds every window that's covering something.

Collapsed windows in the same stack cascade instead of stacking, so every one of them stays clickable.

This is a viewing aid and nothing more. A collapsed window still loads at its full saved size, and the folding is forgotten when you switch profiles.

FancyZones

If you use PowerToys FancyZones, tick "FancyZones" under the canvas and WLM draws the zones it would snap a window into. Drag a window onto one and it lands on that zone exactly. The picker beside the tick box chooses which layout is drawn, and the default is whatever FancyZones itself has applied to that monitor.

Zones are drawn under the windows, so they read as the surface rather than as more windows.

WLM reads PowerToys' layouts and never writes to them. Those files are live — PowerToys keeps them in memory and rewrites them on its own schedule — so editing them from outside would race it and could lose the layouts you built. Zone authoring stays in PowerToys' own editor.

Zones are matched to monitors by hardware id, the same way FancyZones does it. Profiles saved before this release borrow that from whichever monitor is currently in the same role, and pick up their own copy the next time you hit Update.

Precise Edits, Undo & History

The "EDIT" button (list view) or "Edit" panel (visual view) opens exact fields for any window: desktop, monitor, state, the colour it's drawn in, and pixel-perfect position and size. X and Y sit together on one line, width and height on the next, so the pairs read as pairs.

The list itself is a table — app and title, which monitor, which desktop — and hovering a row brings up a card with the rest: a scaled picture of where that window sits on your actual monitor layout, its resolution, its position, and whether it's normal, minimised or maximised.

Every dialog in WLM is a real window rather than a panel drawn over the app, so you can drag this one onto a second monitor, resize it, and keep looking at the layout behind it while you type. It opens a little smaller than the main window and stays wherever you leave it. The app itself is disabled while a dialog is up - that's Windows doing it, not a panel sitting on top - so a stray keystroke can't land in the window behind.

Each dialog knows the size its own layout needs and won't be dragged smaller than that, so there's no size you can resize one to where it stops making sense. The one exception is assign-hotkey, which stays a fixed panel over the app: its whole job is to catch the next key combination you press.

How a window gets recognised

Apps rewrite their titles as you work. An editor names the file you have open, a browser names the page. A window saved with an exact title stops being found the moment it says something else, and WLM falls back to a weaker test that can pick the wrong window of the same app.

"Recognise by", at the bottom of the edit dialog, lets each window choose:

  • App and its exact title - the default, and what WLM has always done.
  • App and a title starting with... - for the part of the title that stays put. "Inbox" still finds "Inbox (3) - Gmail".
  • App and window type, any title - for apps that rename constantly.

The looser two give up the title as a way of telling windows apart, so they require the window class and type to match instead. A rule you set also applies to the last-resort match on the app alone: if you asked for "starts with Inbox" you were being selective on purpose, and handing you any window of that app would undo the rule you just wrote.

  • Ctrl+Z / Ctrl+Y - Undo/redo any edit, with a labeled trail.
  • Ctrl+H or "History" - Browse and restore full saved versions of the profile (every Update keeps a snapshot).
  • Windows tracked live (see Hold mode) also keep a per-window position history you can roll back individually.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial auto-activation

Monitor Binding & Auto-Activation

Bind a profile to your current monitor setup and WLM can restore it by itself whenever that setup reappears: docking the laptop, starting a Parsec session with a virtual display, plugging into the projector.

  • Triggers - On monitor change, at app launch, and/or a dedicated hotkey. All off by default. You opt in.
  • Best-guess remap - When your monitors almost match a saved setup, WLM picks the closest mapping, applies it, and remembers the decision so it never asks twice.
  • The "Status" panel logs every decision auto-activation makes. You can always see why a profile did (or didn't) fire.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial hold

Hold Mode

The "Hold layout" toggle (next to the hotkey chip) pins a profile as the live layout. While holding, WLM watches your windows a few times a second:

  • If an app moves or resizes its own window, it gets put straight back.
  • If you drag a window yourself, WLM records the move into the profile instead of fighting you. Your layout evolves as you work.
  • Loading a different profile releases the hold automatically. Explicit action always wins.
Hold is ideal for apps that "helpfully" reposition themselves after fullscreen games, RDP sessions, or monitor sleep.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial desktops

Virtual Desktops

On Windows 11, profiles capture which virtual desktop each window lives on. Load sends every window back to its desktop, and will create missing desktops if the profile needs more than currently exist. The visual editor shows one tab per desktop. The per-window editor lets you reassign a window to any desktop, or leave it "Unmapped" to stay wherever it is.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial keyboard

Command Palette & Keyboard

Ctrl+K opens the command palette: every action and every profile, fuzzy-searchable.

  • Vim-style navigation in both panes: j/k, gg/G, / filter, Tab to switch panes.
  • Single-key actions on the focused profile: Enter load, r rename, d delete, n new.
  • In the windows pane, j/k move a cursor through the saved windows, Enter edits the one under it and x removes it. Tab into the pane and the cursor is already on a row.
  • Both lists wrap at the ends, and moving through the profile list shows that profile on the right as you go — a preview, not a load, so nothing on your desktop moves.
  • ? shows the complete keyboard reference.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial updates

Updates

WLM checks this site's update feed on launch and on a schedule you control. When a new build is out, a badge will appear in the title bar. One click downloads, verifies the file against its published size and sha256, and swaps the new build in place.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial blacklist

Blacklist

Keep noise out of your captures. Rules match on process name, window class, or title, and the "Block app" button in the visual editor adds one in a click. Blacklisted windows are ignored at save and at load.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial companions

Companion Software

The "Companion software" tab detects complementary tools and offers one-click installs for whatever's missing: PowerToys for live drag-snap zones, Parsec for remote desktop, virtual display drivers for headless monitors, PowerShell 7 for scripting. For the ones you already have it reports the installed version, can ask winget whether there's an update and run it, and can uninstall.

The tab draws immediately and fills in what it found a moment later, rather than making you wait on the scan.

Some software never registers itself in the usual place, so detection also looks at installed drivers and at what's on your PATH. That's how PowerShell 7 shows up as present.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial diagnostics

Diagnostics & Reporting a Bug

The "Diagnostics" tab shows the app version and the exact paths WLM is reading and writing, which is usually the fastest way to work out why something isn't behaving. "Copy to clipboard" grabs the whole dump, and "Open profiles folder" and "Open crash log" take you straight to the files.

"Report a bug" and "Request a feature" open a short form. You'll see exactly what gets sent before anything leaves your machine, the diagnostics dump is optional, and your Windows username is stripped out of the paths. Issues are public, so anything you type there is too.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial window

Window Positions & About

The app window and each kind of dialog remember the size and position you last left them at, and come back to them next launch. A size you chose by dragging always beats the size a dialog would pick for itself.

"Reset window positions" forgets all of it, which is what you want when something has ended up on a monitor you no longer have.

"About" carries the running version, the licence, and where your profiles live.

wlm:~$ wlm --tutorial cli

CLI, Links & Automation

  • WindowLayoutManager.exe --load="Coding" - Load a profile from a script, Task Scheduler job, or Stream Deck button. Works whether or not WLM is already running.
  • wlm://load/Coding - The same, as a clickable link. wlm://show raises the window.
  • Taskbar jump list - Right-click the WLM taskbar icon for one-click profile loads.
  • Post-load hook - A profile's JSON can carry a postLoad command (e.g., switch an OBS scene) that runs after a successful load.
  • WLM lives in the tray. Closing the window keeps hotkeys, hold mode, and auto-activation running.
