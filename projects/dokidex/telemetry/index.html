:: REAL PERFORMANCE DATA

Performance, Measured

Real performance data from one RTX 5090, with plain explanations of where the speed comes from: how fast each model writes, what a mixture-of-experts model buys you, and what the whole thing costs in memory, heat and power.

Beginner

This is performance data. Speed, memory, temperature, power and wait times, recorded automatically from one RTX 5090 across a full day of automated coding work, plus short explanations of where the speed comes from.

Most pages about local AI tell you what a setup can do. This one tells you what this one did.

A snapshot, not a live feed. Measured 25 to 26 August 2026 and frozen there. Records for models the server no longer runs are left out, so nothing below refers to something you could not load today.

The short version

   
Requests handled 1,592
Succeeded all of them
Models exercised 19
Words written roughly 749,000
Words of prompt handled roughly 30,600,000
of which had to be read fresh roughly 4,800,000

749,000 words is about eight novels, written in a day, on hardware that fits under a desk, with nothing leaving the house.

The two prompt rows are the interesting pair. The machine was handed 30.6 million words of context and only had to actually read 4.8 million of them. The other 84% was already in memory from the message before. That gap is the single most important number on this page, and trick 2 below is how it happens.

Not all models run at the same speed, and the gap is enormous

Every model on this machine was given the same prompt and asked the same question. This is the most useful table on the page, because the spread is far wider than most people expect.

gpt-oss-120b (too big for the card) offloaded 21

The everyday model is among the slowest on the box, and that is deliberate.

The quick ones are mixture-of-experts models. The whole model sits in memory, but only a small slice of it does the work for any given word, often about a tenth. That makes them dramatically faster, at the cost of being, loosely speaking, a smaller model wearing a larger model’s coat.

The default here is dense: all 27 billion of its parameters take part in every single word. That is exactly why it is slower, and also why it was picked. It was the only candidate that passed this project’s own coding test, and a faster model that writes worse code is not a faster machine. It is a slower one with extra steps.

The bottom row shows what happens when a model does not fit. gpt-oss-120b spills into ordinary system memory and falls to a tenth of the speed of a model that fits. Fitting on the card matters more than almost anything else.

Three tricks that make the slow choice fast enough

1. Let a small model guess ahead

The big model is slow because it does so much work per word. So a small model guesses the next few words, and the big one checks all those guesses in a single pass, which is far cheaper than writing them itself. Correct guesses are kept for free. Wrong ones are thrown away.

The guesser is right about 53% of the time here, and that is worth roughly 44% more speed. Measured across 1,334 recorded requests: 40 words per second without it, 58 with it.

It costs about 4 GB to keep the guesser loaded, and on a full card that has to come from somewhere.

2. Don’t re-read what you already read

Normally, every time you send a message the model reads the whole conversation again from the start. In a long session that dwarfs everything else. This machine keeps the previous reading and carries on from where it stopped.

At 157,000 tokens deep, a continuing conversation re-reads 33 of them. That is 99.98% of the work skipped, and it is the single reason a long conversation stays usable instead of getting slower every turn.

It stops at the session boundary, and that turns out to be the biggest cost here. Start a new session and the whole thing is read again from scratch. In the data above, 232 requests did exactly that, and between them they account for 87% of all the fresh reading this machine has ever done. Continuing a session is not just convenient, it is most of the performance.

3. Store the conversation in smaller numbers

That kept reading is stored at reduced precision, which quarters its size. The space saved buys a far larger conversation instead.

The result is slightly unintuitive: the current setup holds two and a half times the conversation of the previous one while using less memory overall. Controlled testing put the cost at about three percent of speed, with no quality difference this project’s test suite could detect.

Worth being straight about what this trick is for. It buys room, not speed. Measuring the attention step directly shows the cost tracks how many entries are stored rather than how big each one is, so shrinking them frees memory and returns almost nothing on the clock. That is a good trade here, because fitting on the card is what everything else depends on. It is not a speed optimization dressed up as one.

What it costs the graphics card

   
Card RTX 5090, 32 GB
In use while working 30.1 GB, peaking at 31.2 GB
Temperature 38 °C idle, up to 82 °C working
Power 57 W idle, up to 601 W working
Busy during a request median 73%, peaking at 100%

Working at 31.2 of 32 GB is the constraint behind every other decision here. There is no spare room, which is why the machine runs one thing at a time, why the image and video tools are parked rather than kept resident, and why the guesser in trick 1 had to displace something else to earn its place.

At idle the whole thing draws about as much as a bright light bulb.

How long you actually wait

   
Half of all requests finished within 5.0 seconds
A quarter within 2.2 seconds
A quarter took longer than 11.9 seconds
The longest single request 298.8 seconds

The long tail is not the machine struggling. The longest request wrote 16,100 tokens, about twelve thousand words, in one unbroken go. At that length, five minutes is simply how long it takes to say that much.

What this does not measure

A number without its limits is worth less than no number.

It says nothing about answer quality. Speed and quality are separate questions. Quality is measured by a coding suite run three times over. It was 23 tasks during this snapshot’s window and has since grown to 26, and the models page covers what that found, including where this setup falls short.

Words per second are converted from tokens, at the usual rule that a hundred tokens is about seventy-five words. The underlying measurement is in tokens.

One workload dominates, and it is a test. Almost all of this is an automated coding benchmark: 269 runs of the 23-task suite that ran during this snapshot’s window (26 tasks today), each one a fresh session. That matters for two reasons. Chat would look very different and much faster, because chat does not re-read a codebase every turn. And a benchmark starts a new session every minute or so, which is the worst case for the reuse described above.

Most models were measured once. The per-model table is one careful reading each, not an average of many runs. That is enough to show a threefold gap, and not enough to separate two models a few percent apart.
