:: THE LEARN TRACK

Where to start

Nine pages, no assumed background: from what a token is to why mixture-of-experts changes your VRAM math. Pick your entry point below.

Why this track exists

DokiDex is a local agentic coding stack, and building it meant answering, precisely, the questions that “just call the API” lets you skip. What a token actually is. Why a model that fits in 24 GB of VRAM behaves nothing like one that needs 80. Why the same benchmark score means something different for two different models. Why a chain-of- thought trace isn’t a transcript of what the model “really” did. Those answers didn’t start as a course. They started as notes, checked against papers and vendor docs because a wrong one would have shipped a bug. This track is those notes, reorganized for a reader who wasn’t in the room.

It assumes nothing. Not a CS degree, not a prior brush with machine learning, not even comfort with “model” meaning anything other than what your camera draws a box around.

Start here

Four ways in, depending on where you’re standing:

  • Never touched this before. Start at Foundations. It opens with what a model actually is (not a database it searches) and ends with why models hallucinate. Nine ideas, no prerequisite.
  • You can code, but the ML is a black box. Skip ahead to How models work. It assumes you already know what a token and a prompt are, and goes straight to gradient descent, the transformer block, attention, and the KV cache. Double back to Foundations the moment a term stalls you.
  • You want to run models on your own hardware. Go to Running models locally. It’s the practical end of the track (VRAM math, quantization formats, which inference engine fits your GPU) and links back to the theory only where you actually need it.
  • You want to evaluate models properly. Go to Benchmarks. A leaderboard number is easy to misread and easy to game. This page is about reading one skeptically before you trust it.

Tip: if any page assumes a term you don’t recognize, it’s almost certainly defined earlier in Foundations or in the Glossary.

The nine pages

Three tiers of theory, five reference pages you can read in any order, and a curated list of outside teaching.

  • Tier 1 Foundations Tokens, embeddings, next-token prediction, context windows, temperature, and hallucination: the ideas everything else stands on.
  • Tier 2 How models work Neural networks, gradient descent, the transformer block, attention, the KV cache, and how pretraining, SFT, and RLHF actually differ.
  • Tier 3 Advanced Mixture-of-experts routing, quantization, scaling laws, chain-of-thought, and the open problems in interpretability and alignment.
  • Reference Decoding model names What "Sonnet 4.5" or "GLM-4.6" actually encode, and why the naming conventions differ from vendor to vendor.
  • Reference Open vs closed weights What you can and can't legally do with a model's weights, and why the license line matters more than the benchmark score.
  • Reference Benchmarks What a leaderboard number is actually measuring, where it gets gamed, and how to read one skeptically.
  • Reference Running models locally VRAM math, quantization formats, and the inference engines that make a consumer GPU viable.
  • Reference Coding agents How a tool-using agent loop actually works, and where it can quietly compound its own mistakes.
  • Extras Best videos & courses Longform courses and shortform explainers, vetted against the same sourcing bar as this track.

How the concepts connect

The three tiers aren’t arbitrary buckets. Later concepts genuinely build on earlier ones. The map below keeps only the handful of prerequisite links the track actually leans on. It isn’t an exhaustive graph of everything that touches everything else.

How to read this track

Every page here follows the same rules as the rest of this site. A blockquote that opens with a bold label is a callout, not decoration. Key idea marks the one thing worth remembering. Common trap or Misconception flags a popular belief that’s wrong. Note adds a caveat. Warning means something will actually break if you skip it.

Every factual claim (a benchmark score, a license, a parameter count, a price) carries an inline link to where it came from. Anything that will read differently in six months (a price, a leaderboard rank, a model lineup) is marked “as of July 2026.” Where the field itself hasn’t settled something (whether a chain-of-thought trace is a faithful account of what a model did, whether alignment is a solved problem or an open one), the page says so and points at the evidence on both sides, rather than picking a side to sound more confident than the research supports.
