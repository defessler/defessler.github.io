How models work

Open the box. What a transformer actually does to a token as it flows through the network, why attention is not what most explainers claim it is, and what pretraining, SFT, and RLHF each can and cannot change.

Intermediate

Foundations covered tokens, embeddings, and the idea that a model is just repeatedly predicting the next token. This page opens the box between “input tokens” and “output distribution.” It looks at the machinery that does the predicting: the transformer block, the attention mechanism inside it, the caching trick that makes generation fast enough to use, and the three-stage training pipeline that turns a raw next-token predictor into something you can hold a conversation with.

None of this requires calculus. You need shapes, not proofs: a vector is a list of numbers, a matrix multiply is a structured way of combining two lists of numbers, and “learning” is a long sequence of very small, very repetitive nudges to those numbers.

Neural networks and gradient descent, briefly

A neural network is a stack of layers. Each layer takes in a vector, multiplies it by a matrix of learned weights, adds a learned bias, and passes the result through a nonlinear function (something that bends the output instead of just scaling it. Without this bend, stacking ten layers would mathematically collapse into one layer, because ten linear steps in a row is still just one linear step). Stack enough of these bent layers and the network can approximate extremely complicated functions: not because any single layer is smart, but because composing many simple, bent transformations is a surprisingly powerful trick.

“Training” is the process of finding good values for every weight in every layer. It works like this: run an example forward through the network, compare the output to what you wanted (this gap is the loss), then work backward through every layer computing how much each individual weight contributed to that gap. That backward pass (an application of the chain rule from calculus, mechanized so a computer can do it for billions of weights) is called backpropagation. It tells you the direction each weight should move to reduce the loss a little. Gradient descent is the separate step that actually moves the weight a small amount in that direction, then the whole cycle repeats on the next batch of examples, millions of times. Backpropagation computes the direction. Gradient descent takes the step. They’re often mentioned in the same breath. But they are not the same thing. Backpropagation (Wikipedia)

Key idea: training is not one event. It’s an enormous number of tiny weight nudges, each one computed from how wrong the model’s last guess was on one small batch of examples.

A useful, limited picture: imagine standing on a hillside in fog, feeling which direction slopes downward under your feet, and taking a small step that way, repeatedly, hoping to end up in a low valley. That’s gradient descent. It breaks as a picture almost immediately at real scale: a production model’s “hillside” has billions of dimensions, not two. There’s no single lowest valley to find, only many different valleys that turn out to be roughly equally good. And the step size itself is adjusted over the course of training by a schedule, not held fixed. Don’t lean on the hillside image past “take a small step downhill, repeat.” Everything past that is a different picture.

Two more things worth knowing before the transformer specifics: a modern LLM’s layers aren’t literally “detects cats” style units. Most individual neurons respond to a tangled mix of unrelated concepts (a property researchers call polysemantic), which is exactly why understanding what a trained network is doing usually requires specialized techniques rather than reading a neuron’s activity directly, a research area covered on the Advanced page. Scaling Monosemanticity (Anthropic)

The transformer block: what happens to a token, layer by layer

The repeating unit of a modern LLM is the transformer block. Every block does two things to every token’s representation, in order. First, a self-attention sublayer lets each token position exchange information with every other position in the sequence. Second, a feed-forward sublayer transforms each token’s representation on its own, with no cross-token mixing at all. Both sublayers are wrapped in a residual connection: the sublayer’s output is added to its input rather than replacing it, so information from earlier in the stack has a direct path forward even if a given sublayer contributes little for a particular token. Stack this two-step block dozens of times and you have a modern LLM. Attention Is All You Need (Vaswani et al., 2017)

That “mixes information across positions” line is doing a lot of work, and it’s the part worth slowing down for.

Attention and self-attention

Self-attention is the mechanism that lets a token’s representation be informed by the other tokens in the sequence, weighted by how relevant each one is judged to be, automatically and freshly, at every layer. Mechanically, every token position produces three vectors from its current representation: a Query (what this position is looking for), a Key (what this position offers, for other positions to match against), and a Value (the actual content this position contributes once it’s been matched). A token’s new representation is built by comparing its Query against every position’s Key to get a set of match scores, turning those scores into weights that sum to one, and then taking a weighted blend of every position’s Value using those weights. Attention Is All You Need, The Illustrated Transformer

A library-lookup picture is useful here, carefully bounded: your Query is the question you’re holding, each book’s Key is a short tag you compare your question against, and the Value is the content you actually walk away with, weighted by how well each book’s tag matched. Where the picture breaks matters: a library lookup is one deliberate search by a conscious searcher. Self- attention runs this exact comparison for every token, simultaneously, automatically, inside every layer: there’s no searcher deciding what’s relevant, just a learned scoring function applied uniformly.

One restriction is doing enormous work here, and it’s easy to miss. In a decoder-only model (which every model this site discusses is), attention is causal: a mask blocks each position from attending to any position after it. In “The cat sat down”, the query issued from sat can look at The, cat, and itself, and never at down. The attention matrix is lower-triangular.

That is not a detail. It is what makes next-token training possible at all: if a position could see the tokens that come after it, the answer would leak into the input and the model would learn nothing. It is also what makes the KV cache further down this page valid: cached Keys and Values for earlier positions stay correct precisely because nothing later can change them.

Common trap: “attention lets every token see every other token.” True for encoder models like BERT, false for the generative models people actually mean. Reading a bidirectional picture onto a decoder-only LLM makes both its training and its caching behaviour impossible to explain.

Common trap: “the attention weights show you what the model is paying attention to, or explain why it answered the way it did.” This is the single most common overclaim about transformers. It is not true in the way people mean it. A controlled study specifically testing this found that learned attention weights are frequently uncorrelated with gradient-based measures of what actually influenced a prediction, and that noticeably different attention patterns can be found that produce the same output: meaning the weight pattern does not uniquely determine, and does not reliably reveal, the decision. Attention weights are a mathematically real part of the computation. They are not, on their own, a trustworthy explanation of it. Attention is not Explanation (Jain & Wallace, 2019), arXiv:1902.10186

Two honest caveats on that citation, since this page elsewhere polices exactly this kind of overreach: the study predates transformers (it tested attention in RNN-based classifiers), and it drew a direct published rebuttal, Attention is not not Explanation (Wiegreffe & Pinter, 2019), arguing its adversarial-attention method sets the bar in the wrong place. The conclusion has held up in later work on transformers. It just isn’t settled by this one paper.

The diagram below makes the same point a different way: a full weight matrix for a short toy sentence looks informative (and it’s a real, load-bearing part of the computation), but reading it as “here is the model’s reasoning” goes further than the evidence supports.

Multi-head attention: why not just one attention computation?

Rather than computing attention once, a transformer layer runs several attention computations in parallel, called heads, each with its own independently learned Query, Key, and Value projections, on the same input. The original paper used 8 heads per layer. The outputs of all heads are concatenated and projected back down to the model’s working dimension before moving on to the feed-forward sublayer. Running several smaller attention computations side by side, rather than one large one, lets different heads specialize in picking up on different kinds of relationships between tokens. Attention Is All You Need

Common trap: it’s tempting to assume each head cleanly corresponds to one interpretable linguistic role: “the syntax head,” “the coreference head.” Some heads, on inspection, do show identifiable specialization. That is not guaranteed or universal across heads, layers, or models. Heads are optimized purely to reduce the training loss, and a legible role is a possible emergent outcome, never a designed one. Treat any specific “this head does X” claim about a specific model as an empirical finding about that model, not a general property of multi-head attention itself.

Positional encoding: why order has to be injected on purpose

Self-attention, as described above, is a comparison between every token and every other token: nothing in that comparison inherently knows where any token sits in the sequence. Left unmodified, self-attention would treat a sentence the same as any shuffled ordering of its words. Position has to be added in deliberately. The original transformer did this by adding a fixed wave-shaped (sinusoidal) vector to each token’s embedding based on its position. Most current models instead use Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), which rotates the Query and Key vectors by an angle proportional to their position before the attention comparison happens. Attention Is All You Need; RoFormer / RoPE (Su et al.), arXiv:2104.09864

The reason RoPE is worth naming specifically: rotating both a Query and a Key by the same offset leaves their comparison unchanged. What that means in practice is that RoPE doesn’t give a token an absolute coordinate the model reads off: it encodes relative distance, how far apart two tokens are, directly through the angle between their rotated vectors. A clock-hand picture works for the core idea (comparing two hands tells you a time difference, regardless of what absolute time it is), and it’s worth stopping the picture there. RoPE’s actual rotation happens across many vector dimensions at different frequencies simultaneously, and extending a model to handle sequences longer than it was trained on (a technique called YaRN, among others) specifically means reworking those rotation frequencies, which is Advanced-tier material. RoFormer, arXiv:2104.09864

Misconception: “position is a tag added once, and the model reads it off like a page number.” What actually gets used in the comparison is relative distance between two positions, not an absolute address (a subtle but important difference), because it’s why stretching a model past its trained context length is a real engineering problem rather than a non-issue.

The KV cache: why generation would be quadratic without it

Generation happens one token at a time: compute a distribution over the vocabulary, sample a token, append it, repeat. Naively, computing attention for the newest token would mean recomputing Keys and Values for every previous token in the sequence, at every layer, on every single step, work that grows with the square of the sequence length as the conversation gets longer.

The fix is the KV cache: once a token’s Key and Value vectors are computed at a given layer, they’re stored. Generating the next token only requires computing a fresh Query for the new position and comparing it against the already-cached Keys and Values: no recomputation of anything earlier. This turns what would be roughly quadratic work into linear, incremental work per step.

Common trap: “the KV cache is the model’s memory of the conversation.” It’s purely a compute-saving mechanism tied one-to-one to the current context window, not anything like human memory. It holds no meaning on its own. It’s thrown away when the request ends unless the server is deliberately holding a shared prefix for reuse, and its size grows linearly with how much text has been generated. A model advertising a one-million-token context window (Claude Opus 5, Sonnet 5, and Fable 5 all ship this as of July 2026) can in principle hold a correspondingly enormous cache, which is precisely why serving very long contexts is as much a memory-engineering problem as a modeling one. That tradeoff, and the techniques used to shrink it (grouped-query attention, compressed latent caches), is exactly what Running models locally turns into a concrete VRAM budget, and what Advanced covers mechanically. Three training stages: pretraining, SFT, and RLHF

A released chat model is the product of three distinct training stages, each changing something different, and none of them able to fix what the others got wrong.

Stage What it does What it changes What it cannot fix
Pretraining Next-token prediction over a massive raw text/code corpus Builds broad world knowledge and language ability from scratch Doesn’t teach the model to follow instructions rather than just continue text
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) Further training on curated (prompt, ideal-response) demonstration pairs Teaches instruction-following behavior and response format Doesn’t reliably add new facts the pretraining data didn’t contain
RLHF A reward model trained on human rankings of outputs, used to further optimize the policy Reshapes style, helpfulness, tone, and preference-alignment Doesn’t make the model “know more”: it’s a preference effect, not a knowledge effect

Common trap: “RLHF is what makes the model smarter or more accurate.” The foundational RLHF paper’s headline finding is specifically a preference effect: a 1.3B-parameter model trained with RLHF produced outputs human raters preferred over a 175B-parameter base model with no RLHF, because RLHF reshapes style, helpfulness, and instruction-following, not the underlying factual knowledge laid down in pretraining. Fine-tuning in general is much more reliable at changing behavior than at reliably injecting new, precise facts. If you need a model to use specific, current information, retrieval (below) is the more dependable tool. Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback (Ouyang et al., “InstructGPT”), arXiv:2203.02155

Instruction following, system prompts, and chat templates

Instruction following (the ability to read “write a haiku about rain” and actually write a haiku, instead of continuing the sentence with more instructions) is primarily an SFT/RLHF product, not a pretraining one. A raw pretrained (“base”) model is just as likely to continue a list of instructions as to follow the first one, because both patterns appear in its training data. Instruction-following specifically has to be trained in. InstructGPT, arXiv:2203.02155

A system prompt is a block of instructions (persona, rules, available tools, formatting constraints) placed ahead of the user’s messages that the model is trained to weight more durably than an ordinary turn. It’s a strong steering signal, not a hard sandbox boundary: a system prompt is still just text the model attends to, and determined adversarial prompting can sometimes get a model to deviate from or leak it. Treat it as raising the bar substantially, not as an unbreakable rule enforced outside the model.

A chat template is the exact string format (specific role markers, specific special tokens, specific whitespace) that a given model’s training pipeline used to represent a conversation. Every model family is trained on one particular template. Using a different, “reasonable-looking” format (say, plain User: / Bot: labels) instead of the model’s actual template is a common, silent way to degrade a self-hosted model’s output.

Note: a chat-template mismatch usually fails quietly. A garbled printer driver fails loudly: a blank or scrambled page. A wrong chat template doesn’t: the model still produces fluent, plausible-looking text, just measurably worse than it would with the correct template, which is exactly why it’s easy to miss when running an open model locally and blame the model itself instead of the formatting. Running models locally covers the practical side of getting this right.

Function calling: how a text model “calls” anything at all

A model has no way to directly run code. It only ever generates text. Tool use works by having the calling application describe available functions (name, and a schema for the arguments) to the model up front. Mid-response, the model can emit a structured request (a tool name plus JSON arguments matching that schema) instead of ordinary prose. The application reads that request, actually executes the real function, and sends the result back as a new message, which the model then incorporates into what it says next. Tool use with Claude

Note: most tools run on the caller’s own infrastructure (your code, your servers), but a handful of server-run tools (Anthropic’s own web search, for example) execute on the provider’s infrastructure instead. The distinction matters for what you’re actually trusting when a tool result comes back. Tool use with Claude

RAG and vector search: retrieval is not memory

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pairs a pretrained generator with a search step: at query time, the query is embedded (Foundations covered embeddings) and compared against a vector index of candidate passages. The closest matches are inserted directly into the prompt, so generation is conditioned on that retrieved text rather than purely on whatever the model happened to absorb during pretraining. The original RAG paper found this measurably produces more specific and factual language than a purely parametric baseline. Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks (Lewis et al., 2020), arXiv:2005.11401

Two things RAG is often described as, that it isn’t. First, it’s not giving the model memory. Retrieved passages are inserted into the same context window covered in Foundations, on this one call, and vanish exactly like everything else in the context once the request ends. Nothing about RAG changes the model’s weights or gives it a persistent store of anything. Second, it doesn’t eliminate hallucination. It changes what information is available to condition on, which reduces one specific failure mode (inventing facts with no source at all), but the model can still misread, ignore, or misquote a retrieved passage. And the retriever itself can hand back an irrelevant or wrong passage in the first place.

Common trap: “RAG gives the model memory” and “RAG solves hallucination” are two separate overclaims that tend to travel together. RAG is a retrieval step feeding text into the prompt, nothing more. It shifts which failure modes are likely. It doesn’t remove failure. Handing a model the right source doesn’t guarantee it reads that source correctly. And if the retriever hands back the wrong source, having “citations” at all can make a wrong answer look more credible, not less. RAG, arXiv:2005.11401

Evaluation basics

A benchmark score is a number produced by running a model against a fixed set of test questions under a fixed scoring rule. It’s only as meaningful as that construction. Two cautions carry over directly from Foundations’ discussion of hallucination: a scoring rule that credits a confident wrong answer the same as an honest “I’m not sure” (both zero) structurally rewards guessing over calibrated uncertainty, so a model can be shaped to score better on an eval in ways that make it a worse, more overconfident conversational partner in practice. Why Language Models Hallucinate, arXiv:2509.04664

Benchmarks have also driven real, useful course-corrections: comparisons showing that a 280-billion-parameter model (Gopher) was undertrained relative to its size, and that a properly data-matched, far smaller 70B model (Chinchilla) beat it at the same training compute budget, are what shifted the field away from treating raw parameter count as a quality proxy on its own. Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models (“Chinchilla”), arXiv:2203.15556

This page only needs you to know evaluation exists and what it’s for. The actual landscape of benchmarks (what they measure, where the well-known ones disagree, and how to read a leaderboard without being misled by it) is its own page: Benchmarks.

What’s next

You now have the mechanical picture: tokens flow through stacked attention-plus-feed-forward blocks, and attention is a real but non-explanatory weighting mechanism. Position and the KV cache are both engineering answers to gaps in the base mechanism, and three distinct training stages shaped what the model in front of you actually does.
