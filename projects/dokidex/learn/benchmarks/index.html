:: BENCHMARKS

Benchmarks: how to read an AI score honestly

What the major AI benchmarks actually measure, where every current leaderboard lives, and how to tell a real capability gain from a scaffolding trick, a contaminated dataset, or a footnote doing the heavy lifting.

IntermediateAdvanced

Every model launch comes with a wall of percentages. This page has two jobs. First, to teach you to read one of those numbers the way a skeptical reviewer would: what it measures, what makes it move besides the thing it claims to measure, and when to distrust it outright. Second, to be the link directory you actually want: a direct path from every benchmark name you’ll see quoted to its real leaderboard, so you can check the current number yourself instead of trusting a screenshot in a press release.

Everything below is sourced and dated. Leaderboards move. This snapshot is as of July 2026. Check the date on the live page before you repeat a number from here in six months.

Key idea: A benchmark score is not a fact about a model. It’s a fact about a model, a prompt template, a tool-access setting, a reasoning-effort tier, and an attempt-counting rule. Change any one of those and the number moves, often by more than the gap between two competing models.

Part 0: The short answer

The rest of this page teaches you to distrust benchmark numbers. That’s the right instinct. This section is where it gets applied rather than used as an excuse not to answer. As of July 2026, with the evidence in Part 3.

The honest headline isn’t a winner. It’s this:

Key idea: the top is compressed and the prices are not. Five models land between 93% and 97% on SWE-bench Verified (a 4.0-point spread, inside what scaffolding and prompt differences alone can move) while their input prices run from $1 to $10 per MTok, a 10× range. The useful question is not “which model is best” but “how little can I pay to stay inside that band.”

So read the table below as shortlists with a reason, not as rankings. Where the gap between first and second is smaller than the noise, that’s said outright rather than hidden behind a bolded name.

A single vendor-reported number with nothing to compare it against is a selection artifact, not evidence that a model is best at something.

What holds up across more than one evaluator

Two claims (and only two) survive being checked against genuinely separate sources:

  • Anthropic’s line leads on coding and on human preference. Opus 5 tops SWE-bench Verified (vals.ai), Fable 5 tops human-preference voting (arena.ai), and Opus 5 tops a third, methodologically unrelated composite (Artificial Analysis, 61). Note that different Anthropic models win different things. This is a family result, not one model sweeping. A caution on how this used to be argued here: the Vals Index isn’t independent corroboration of the SWE-bench result, because vals.ai produces both and the composite contains SWE-bench. Two legs from one evaluator is one leg.
  • Price varies far more than measured capability. Inside the leading band, input runs $1 to $10 per MTok (10×) and output $6 to $50 (8.3×), while the capability spread is a few points on benchmarks that no longer discriminate. That asymmetry is visible on every pricing page and every leaderboard. It’s the most actionable fact on this page.

Two further claims are worth knowing but rest on one source each. They’re stated as such rather than dressed up as consensus: that marketing tiers don’t track measured capability (Fable 5 is marketed as most capable and scores 60 against Opus 5’s 61 on one index, a 1-point gap, itself inside the noise), and that at least one “frontier” family sits below its class median (the Nova Premier finding, which lives on Open vs closed, not on this page).

Where the honest answer is “nobody knows”

  • The best open-weight model for coding. The highest score below the closed top three belongs to Kimi K3 (93.4%). But its weights are not released. Moonshot’s model page shows an upcoming-release countdown dated 2026-07-27. Until the weights land with a licence attached, it’s a model you can call, not one you can download. Part 3’s table contains no open-weight model at all. So this page can’t tell you what the strongest genuinely runnable open model scores. That’s a gap in the evidence, not a small one.
  • ARC-AGI-2 rankings. One data point isn’t a leaderboard.
  • Anything measured only by its own vendor. The GPQA and ARC-AGI-2 figures above are vendor-reported with no independent re-run: the category Part 4 tells you to discount.

Note: a cost catch that runs the opposite way to most people’s intuition. Claude models from 4.7 onward use a tokenizer producing roughly 30% more tokens for the same text than earlier Claude generations (Anthropic). That’s an intra-vendor comparison, not a claim about OpenAI’s or Google’s tokenizers. Its effect on a like-for-like bill is to make the newer Claude models more expensive than their sticker price suggests, not less. So where this page compares a Claude price against a non-Claude one, the real-world gap is if anything wider than the numbers shown.

This section ages faster than anything else on the site. Leaderboards move weekly. The K3 countdown above expires the day after this was written. Part 2 links every live leaderboard so you can re-check rather than trust this snapshot.

Part 1: The benchmark catalog

Coding and software engineering

SWE-bench / SWE-bench Verified

What it measures. Given a real GitHub repository at a real commit, plus a real issue description, the model must produce a patch. Scoring runs the repository’s own test suite: previously-failing tests that are specific to the issue (FAIL_TO_PASS) must now pass. Previously-passing tests (PASS_TO_PASS) must not regress. github.com/princeton-nlp/SWE-bench

Verified subset. A hand-filtered 500-problem subset, curated with OpenAI’s Preparedness team specifically to drop issues that turned out to be unsolvable, underspecified, or dependent on flaky or unfair tests. The 500-problem count is the one figure here confirmed directly against the repository page. github.com/princeton-nlp/SWE-bench

Failure modes. Solution text can leak into an issue description or a linked PR comment (a documented contamination vector for any benchmark scraped from public pull requests). Tests can be flaky. And scaffolding (the agent harness wrapped around the base model) materially changes the score independent of the model itself: the same weights can score very differently under a minimal harness (mini-SWE-agent) versus a custom one, so a cross-vendor comparison of SWE-bench numbers implicitly compares harnesses as much as models. See Coding agents for what a harness actually contributes.

Saturation. Effectively saturated at the frontier. See the standings table below: the top handful of models sit within about 8 points of each other, most above 93%.

SWE-bench Pro

What it measures. The same patch-and-test loop, but harder, larger, and partly non-public: 1,865 problems across 41 repositories, split into public, held-out, and commercial (private) sets specifically to blunt contamination. morphllm.com/swe-bench-pro

The clearest “which number do you mean” case in this whole page. At least five different “current best” SWE-bench Pro numbers were circulating in this research at once, each real for a specific split:

Sources for the five bars:

  • Claude Opus 5 / Fable 5 / Mythos 5 at 79.2 to 80.3%, per benchlm.ai
  • Claude Opus 4.8 at 69.2%, an aggregate figure from llm-stats.com whose split couldn’t be confirmed
  • GPT-5.4 (xHigh) at 59.1% on Scale AI’s standardized public split, via labs.scale.com
  • Gemini 3.1 Pro at 54.2%, public split, single attempt, from Google’s own model card
  • Claude Opus 4.6 at 47.1% on the private/commercial split, the hardest of the three

Common trap: Quoting “SWE-bench Pro: 80%” without saying which split turns a real, sourced number into something closer to a marketing claim. Always ask: public, held-out, or private/commercial?

Saturation. Not saturated. The private/commercial split in particular still sits well under 50% for good models, in sharp contrast to Verified’s high-90s ceiling.

Terminal-Bench (1.0 / 2.0 / 2.1)

What it measures. An agent operating a real sandboxed terminal (not a chat window) on tasks spanning sysadmin, security, ML, and data-science work: building a kernel, configuring a server, training a model. Scoring is binary pass/fail against defined output checks. tbench.ai

Structure. Version 1.0 shipped 80 tasks. 2.0/2.1 expanded to 89+ higher-quality tasks, plus spin-off suites (Terminal-Bench Science, Terminal-Bench Challenges). tbench.ai

Scaffold sensitivity is severe here. Even setting aside one aggregator entry this research flagged as unreliable (see the warning below), the remaining sourced numbers for nominally similar model tiers span roughly 68% to 92%:

  • Google’s own card reports Gemini 3.1 Pro at 68.5% under a named harness, “Terminus-2” deepmind.google
  • An llm-stats.com tracker puts GPT-5.5 at 82.7% across 49 evaluated models llm-stats.com
  • benchlm.ai reports GPT-5.6 Sol at 91.9% benchlm.ai
  • codingfleet.com’s 2.1 tracker lists Kimi K3 at 88.3% codingfleet.com

None of those four sources name a harness except Google’s, which is itself a clue about which numbers to trust more.

Warning: While assembling this page, one Terminal-Bench aggregator entry turned out, on inspection, to reuse a completely different metric (a model’s output tokens-per-second) as if it were that model’s benchmark pass rate, apparently because both numbers happened to land in a similarly-named column. It has been dropped from this page rather than published. Treat any scraped benchmark table as needing a sanity check against the benchmark’s own definition, not just against the column header.

Saturation. Not clearly saturated given the spread, but the top of the range (>90%) suggests the ceiling is being approached under generous scaffolding. “Generous scaffolding” is the operative, unmeasured variable.

LiveCodeBench

What it measures. Competitive-programming problems continuously scraped from LeetCode, AtCoder, and Codeforces, each tagged with a release date so evaluators can score only on problems published after a model’s training cutoff (a contamination control built into the dataset itself, rather than bolted on after the fact). llm-stats.com

Current top (one tracker, 73 models, dated ~July 2026): DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Max 93.5%, DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Max 91.6%, DeepSeek-V3.2 (Thinking) 83.3%, MiniMax M2 83.0%, LongCat-Flash-Thinking 82.8%, Nemotron 3 Super 81.2%, Grok-3 Mini 80.4%, Grok 4 Fast 80.0%, Grok-4 Heavy 79.4%. llm-stats.com

None of Claude Fable/Opus 5, GPT-5.6, or Gemini 3.1 Pro appear in this tracker’s top 10. Most likely a submission-coverage gap in this one aggregator, not evidence those labs are weaker here. Gemini 3.1 Pro’s own card reports a different, harder variant, “LiveCodeBench Pro,” scored in Elo (2887) rather than percent-correct. Not comparable to the numbers above at all. deepmind.google

Saturation. Moderate. The average across all 73 tracked models is 53.6%, well below the 93.5% leader. The benchmark still discriminates broadly, even as the open-weight frontier (DeepSeek, MiniMax) bunches near the top.

Aider polyglot benchmark

What it measures. 225 Exercism coding exercises across C++, Go, Java, JavaScript, Python, and Rust, scored on a two-stage pass rate (first attempt, then a refinement pass), tracking well-formed-edit-format compliance and cost per run alongside correctness. aider.chat/docs/leaderboards

Verified directly: this leaderboard is stale. Its own footer states last updated November 20,

  1. Its top entries: gpt-5 (high) 88.0%, gpt-5 (medium) 86.7%, o3-pro (high) 84.9%, and Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview 83.1%. These predate the entire current model generation (Fable 5, Opus 5, GPT-5.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro all shipped after this snapshot). aider.chat/docs/leaderboards As of July 2026, use this leaderboard for historical trend context only, never for a current ranking.

HumanEval and MBPP: why they’re dead

What they measured. HumanEval: 164 hand-written Python problems with docstrings and unit tests (an average of 7.7 tests per problem), testing basic algorithmic code generation. MBPP is the same generation-era sibling, similarly small, similarly public since roughly 2021. klu.ai

Why dead, in three parts.

  1. Saturation. By mid-2024, models were already near-ceiling: GPT-4o via the LDB scaffold hit 98.2% pass@1, GPT-4 reached 96.9%, and even Claude 3 Opus scored 84.9%. That left no headroom to discriminate frontier models. klu.ai
  2. Contamination. The problem sets have been public on GitHub for years: a well-recognized risk that current models have simply memorized them during pretraining.
  3. Narrow construct validity. Problems are short, interview-style, and purely algorithmic. They say nothing about repo-scale work, multi-file reasoning, code review, or test-writing.

Corroborating evidence. Neither Claude Opus 5’s nor Gemini 3.1 Pro’s current model card lists a HumanEval or MBPP score at all: the frontier labs themselves have stopped reporting them, which is itself informative. anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-5 · deepmind.google

Knowledge and reasoning

GPQA Diamond

What it measures. 198 multiple-choice questions in biology, physics, and chemistry: the “Diamond” subset is the hardest slice of the 448-question full GPQA set, written and vetted by domain PhDs specifically to be “Google-proof” (resistant to a skilled non-expert doing a web search). llm-stats.com

Human baseline. PhD-level experts in the relevant domain score around 65% raw, rising to about 74% after excluding questions with identified errors in the answer key. The ceiling itself has a documented error rate, a known criticism of the dataset. llm-stats.com

Current top. GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Mythos Preview tied at 94.6% (no tools), per one tracker. llm-stats.com Google’s own Gemini 3.1 Pro card claims 94.3% and calls this “the highest GPQA Diamond score ever reported,” true at its February 2026 launch, since edged out by the newer Sol/Mythos generation. deepmind.google

Verification status. Zero of 232 tracked models on llm-stats.com carry an independently re-run GPQA Diamond result: every number above, including the ones just cited, is vendor self-reported. llm-stats.com

Saturation. Effectively saturated: leading models now score above the expert human baseline, which raises a real question of whether remaining gains reflect genuine reasoning improvement or exploitation of the dataset’s own known answer-key errors.

AIME (and why HMMT matters)

What it measures. All 30 problems from a given year’s American Invitational Mathematics Examination (AIME I + II), integer answers 0 to 999, olympiad-level. llm-stats.com

Fully saturated on the 2025 paper. Five models hit a perfect 1.000: Grok-4 Heavy, GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Pro, GPT-5.2 Pro, and the open-weight Kimi K2-Thinking-0905, with Claude Opus 4.6 close behind at 0.998. llm-stats.com

Why HMMT (Harvard-MIT Math Tournament) gets used instead. Once a given AIME year saturates, evaluators rotate to the freshest available contest specifically because a brand-new contest has had zero time to leak into a training corpus. Freshness (not the benchmark’s brand name) is what does the contamination-resistance work here. The same pattern holds for every contest-math benchmark in this catalog.

A specific, current 2026 HMMT leaderboard couldn’t be located for this page. Treat any claimed current HMMT 2026 score as unverified until you check it against a live source yourself.

FrontierMath

What it measures. Several hundred original, unpublished research-adjacent math problems across four difficulty tiers (undergraduate through open-research-level), authored and peer-reviewed by professional mathematicians, each typically requiring hours of expert effort. epoch.ai

Anti-contamination design. Of 338 total problems, only 12 are public. Unless stated otherwise, every reported number corresponds to evaluation on the private sets. epoch.ai

A page that contradicts its own table. One tracker’s descriptive prose still says current models “solve under 2% of problems,” almost certainly stale boilerplate copied from FrontierMath’s original 2024 launch commentary (describing GPT-4-era models) and never updated. That same page’s own summary statistics, elsewhere on the page, put the leader at 0.9 (90%) and the average at 0.3 (30%) across the 17 models it tracks, consistent with its own per-model rows, where GPT-5.6 Sol sits at 89.0%. llm-stats.com Trust the table over the prose, and treat the “under 2%” line as an artifact of an unmaintained page rather than a current fact.

Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE)

What it measures. 2,500 expert-written multiple-choice and short-answer questions across math, humanities, and natural sciences, explicitly built as a successor once MMLU-class benchmarks saturated. llm-stats.com

The largest cross-source discrepancy on this whole page. Three sources, three dates, three very different pictures:

ARC-AGI-1, ARC-AGI-2, ARC-AGI-3

ARC-AGI-1 measures abstract visual-puzzle generalization on static grids, designed to resist memorization through novel task composition. It is now considered saturated by its own creators. OpenAI’s o3 (December 2024) scored 75.7% on the semi-private eval at a “high-efficiency” compute tier and 87.5% at a “low-efficiency” tier using roughly 172x more compute. ARC Prize’s own post states plainly that “ARC-AGI-1 is now saturating.” It also notes that a large ensemble of cheap solutions can independently reach 81% on the private eval, precisely what motivated building ARC-AGI-2. arcprize.org

ARC-AGI-2 is the harder static-puzzle successor. A June 2025 ARC Prize post found “no clear winner” across every major reasoning system tested at that point. By 2026 the frontier moved substantially: Gemini 3.1 Pro’s own card reports 77.1% under ARC Prize’s own Verified methodology, a large jump from the “no clear winner” era. deepmind.google

ARC-AGI-3, announced March 25, 2026, is a different construct entirely: instead of static, one-shot puzzles, agents operate inside interactive game environments, must acquire goals on the fly, build world models, and adapt continuously, scored across time, not just a final answer, against a 100%-human-efficiency baseline. arcprize.org/arc-agi/3 Anthropic’s Opus 5 announcement claims its score is “three times as high as the next-best model” on ARC-AGI-3 but doesn’t publish the absolute percentage. Treat that comparison as directionally sourced, the absolute number as unverified. anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-5

Practical implication. “ARC-AGI” without a version number is now ambiguous between three benchmarks measuring three different things: saturated static puzzles (v1), active static puzzles (v2), and brand-new interactive agentic learning (v3).

Broad knowledge

MMLU and MMLU-Pro

MMLU (original). The 57-subject multiple-choice benchmark that defined “broad knowledge” evaluation for years, now considered saturated, with frontier models exceeding 90% accuracy, the framing used to justify building HLE in the first place. llm-stats.com

MMLU-Pro, the harder successor: options expanded from 4 to 10 per question, trivial questions removed, and the set skewed toward reasoning-heavy items across 12,000+ questions in 14 domains. Chain-of-thought prompting measurably helps on MMLU-Pro where it didn’t reliably help on plain MMLU. Scores drop 16 to 33 points relative to MMLU on the same models: direct evidence the harder version still discriminates. llm-stats.com

Current top (one tracker, 129 tracked models): Qwen3.7 Max 89.6%, Qwen3.6 Plus 88.5%, Qwen3.7-Plus 88.5%, MiniMax M2.1 88.0%, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B 87.8%. llm-stats.com No Claude/GPT/Gemini flagship appears in this specific top five: most likely a coverage gap in this one tracker rather than a capability gap, the same caveat as LiveCodeBench above.

Saturation. Not saturated per this source’s own framing: meaningful score spread remains across all 129 tracked models.

MMMU (and MMMU-Pro)

What it measures. 11,500 college-level multimodal questions (charts, diagrams, chemical structures) across six disciplines, 30 subjects, and 183 subfields, genuinely requiring image and text reasoning together rather than OCR-plus-guessing. llm-stats.com

Current top (one tracker): Qwen3.6 Plus 86.0%, GPT-5.1 (three variants, tied) 85.4%, GPT-5 84.2%, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B 83.9%, o3 82.9%, Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview 82.0%. llm-stats.com The same coverage caveat applies: no Fable 5, Opus 5, GPT-5.6, or Gemini 3.1 Pro visible in this top ten.

Google separately reports Gemini 3.1 Pro at 80.5% on “MMMU-Pro,” a distinct, harder variant, no tools, not directly comparable to the plain-MMMU numbers above. The “Pro” suffix is doing real work here, not just marketing. deepmind.google

Saturation. Moderate: leader at 86.0%, average 67.3% across 63 tracked models, so real spread remains even though the top cluster (82% to 86%) is tight.

Agentic and tool-use benchmarks

tau-bench and tau2-bench

tau-bench (Sierra Research): agents must complete tasks in airline and retail domains by calling tools correctly while conversing with a simulated user, under written policy constraints. Its signature contribution is the pass^k metric (the success rate when the same task is attempted k times), built specifically to expose reliability problems a single pass/fail run hides. In the original paper, gpt-4o succeeded on under 50% of tasks on a single attempt. Its pass^8 (eight repeated attempts) fell below 25% on retail. A model that “passes” a task once often can’t repeat that success reliably. arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045

tau2-bench adds a Telecom domain and, critically, lets the simulated user modify shared world state too: a customer performing a phone-side action themselves rather than only requesting it. Performance drops significantly moving from a no-user to a dual-control setting. The framework separates reasoning failures from communication failures. arxiv.org/abs/2506.07982

Conflicting current numbers. One llm-stats.com tracker (~July 2026) shows Step-3.5-Flash leading at 88.2%, then GLM-4.7 87.4%, MiMo-V2-Flash 80.3%, GLM-4.7-Flash 79.5%, MiniMax M2 77.2%, with o3 trailing at 63.0%. llm-stats.com That’s dramatically lower than Google’s own Gemini 3.1 Pro card, which reports 90.8% on τ2-bench Retail and 99.3% on τ2-bench Telecom. deepmind.google The gap strongly suggests the llm-stats.com tracker is scoring the older, harder-for-agents original tau-bench (or a different split) rather than τ2-bench proper. This is flagged as unresolved. Do not merge these two number sets.

Artificial Analysis independently builds “τ³-Banking” as one input into its own composite index, a third variant in the same family, again not directly comparable to the two above. artificialanalysis.ai

BFCL (Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard)

What it measures. Accuracy of function/tool-call generation, checked via Abstract Syntax Tree comparison against ground truth, across simple, multiple, parallel, and relevance-detection categories in Python, Java, JavaScript, and REST-API contexts. gorilla.cs.berkeley.edu

Current standings: unverified, and a genuinely useful cautionary tale. The official Berkeley leaderboard is JavaScript-rendered and returned no tabular data to automated fetching. The one aggregator found with BFCL numbers shows a table topped by Llama 3.1 405B/70B/8B Instruct (models from mid-2024) despite the page’s own metadata claiming “last updated July 26, 2026.” llm-stats.com This is the clearest concrete proof found anywhere in this research that a tracker’s “last updated” timestamp can’t be trusted at face value. Do not cite a current BFCL number from a scraped copy. Check Berkeley’s live, JS-rendered site directly.

Composite and index benchmarks

LMArena / Arena.ai (formerly Chatbot Arena)

What it measures. Human pairwise preference votes between two anonymized model outputs, aggregated into an Elo-style rating, across Text, Agent, Code, and Vision categories. lmarena.ai now redirects to arena.ai. arena.ai/leaderboard/text

Methodology, as stated live. Rankings are drawn from 7,430,560 votes across 378 models in the “Overall” Text category, with filters for factuality standards, license type, pricing, and context length. Scores carry ± confidence intervals and a “High confidence” vs. “Preliminary” label depending on vote volume. Data current through July 21, 2026. arena.ai/leaderboard/text

What Elo does and doesn’t measure. Raw stylistic preference under blind pairwise comparison, on whatever prompts real site visitors happen to type. It’s not a correctness benchmark. It’s well documented that longer, more confident-sounding, more pleasantly formatted answers tend to win votes independent of factual accuracy. The site’s own use of confidence intervals and a “Preliminary” label is itself an acknowledgment that vote-driven rankings are statistically noisy, especially for newly added models. Use Arena rank as a proxy for “what will feel good in a chat product,” not for “which model gets a hard task right.”

Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index

What it is. A composite score (v4.1 at the time of this research) built by weighting nine underlying evaluations: GDPval-AA v2, τ³-Banking, Terminal-Bench v2.1, SciCode, Humanity’s Last Exam, GPQA Diamond, CritPt, AA-Omniscience, and AA-LCR (long-context reasoning). artificialanalysis.ai

A footnote that changes cost, not score. The site explicitly notes Anthropic charges a separate cache-write fee on top of normal input pricing, while OpenAI and DeepSeek “typically charge only cache-hit pricing with no write or storage fee,” a structural pricing difference that changes effective cost-per-task independent of any benchmark result. artificialanalysis.ai

Note: Artificial Analysis’s own site states different totals for “models tracked” depending on the view (586 listed overall, 170 evaluated on the Intelligence Index at the time of this research). Any leaderboard rank you see quoted as “#N of M” should be treated as approximate on the denominator, not a fixed site-wide figure.

Vals.ai (independent third-party evaluator)

What it is. An organization that runs its own evaluations rather than relying on vendor self-reports: “We run all of our own evaluations and create many of our benchmarks in-house.” vals.ai It tracks SWE-bench, Terminal-Bench 2.1, LiveCodeBench, GPQA Diamond, MMLU Pro, and MMMU alongside a set of original in-house benchmarks, rolled up into a composite “Vals Index.”

Part 4: Reading a benchmark number honestly

This is the part worth reading even if you skip everything else.

Why scores are systematically overstated

  1. Contamination. Any benchmark built from public data (GitHub PRs, contest archives, textbook question banks) risks being seen, verbatim or near-verbatim, during pretraining. This is the reason HumanEval and MBPP are dead, and why FrontierMath, LiveCodeBench, and fresh-year AIME/HMMT all deliberately hold out or time-window their problems.
  2. Unreported or mismatched scaffolding. The Terminal-Bench spread alone (roughly 68% to 92% for supposedly similar model tiers) shows that the agent harness wrapped around a model can move the number more than the underlying weights do. A bare model name with no stated harness is an incomplete citation. See Coding agents for what a harness actually is.
  3. Best-of-n / pass@k inflation, and its inverse. Some vendor numbers reflect a single attempt (Google’s own card explicitly labels its SWE-bench Verified/Pro numbers “single attempt”) while others reflect majority voting or the best of several tries. tau-bench’s own designers built the pass^k metric specifically because pass@1 numbers hide catastrophic reliability drops under repetition (gpt-4o: under 50% pass@1, under 25% pass^8 on retail). A “% solved” number without stating attempts-per-task and the aggregation rule isn’t comparable across vendors.
  4. Effort-tier and “extended thinking” footnotes. Artificial Analysis’s own table shows the same model, Claude Opus 5, scoring 61 at “Max effort” versus 59 at “High effort,” a nontrivial swing from a setting, not a different model. Vendor cards frequently headline only the highest-effort configuration while shipping a lower default. See Decoding model names for how these tiers show up in the product names themselves.
  5. Selective benchmark reporting. Anthropic’s Opus 5 launch announcement contains zero SWE-bench, Terminal-Bench, or GPQA numbers. It leads with internal, proprietary comparisons instead. This is rational vendor behavior: once a benchmark saturates, or a competitor wins it, marketing shifts to a newer or proprietary yardstick where the story is more favorable.
  6. Aggregator data quality is its own hazard. This research turned up concrete cases of trackers whose “last updated” timestamp didn’t match their actual data (BFCL showing 2024-era Llama 3.1 numbers under a claimed “July 26, 2026” update) and a page whose own prose contradicted its own table (FrontierMath). Treat every third-party number as needing corroboration, not just every vendor number.

The footnotes that change what a score means

Why Elo/arena rankings measure something different from capability

Arena-style Elo is built from blind human pairwise preference votes on whatever prompts real users happen to submit. It captures stylistic and conversational preference (tone, formatting, confidence, response length, perceived helpfulness on everyday queries), not verified correctness on hard, well-specified tasks. It’s well documented in the broader field that longer, more assertive, more nicely formatted answers tend to win votes even when a shorter or more hedged answer is more accurate. Arena’s own confidence intervals and “Preliminary” labels are an acknowledgment that vote-driven rankings are statistically noisy, especially for newly added models. Use Arena rank as a proxy for “what will feel good in a chat product,” not “which model will get a hard software-engineering task right.”

What agentic benchmarks do and don’t tell you about real repository work

They do tell you whether a model, wrapped in a particular scaffold, can complete this specific distribution of self-contained, verifiably-scored tasks: patch-and-test for SWE-bench, command-and-check for Terminal-Bench, tool-call-and-verify for tau-bench and BFCL.

They generally do not tell you about:

  • Reliability under repetition, unless the benchmark explicitly reports pass^k, which most don’t. tau-bench/tau2-bench are the notable exception.
  • Performance on genuinely novel, larger, messier real repositories than the benchmark’s own source corpus.
  • Cost and latency at your actual usage volume: benchmark cost-per-task figures run under research conditions, not production traffic.
  • Behavior on ambiguous or underspecified real tickets: SWE-bench Verified explicitly removed such cases. The real world doesn’t remove them.
  • Multi-day or multi-session task continuity: every benchmark on this page is a single-session run.

The largest scaffold-sensitivity finding on this page (Terminal-Bench’s roughly 60% to 92% spread for similar-tier models) is itself direct evidence that the agent framework around a model does a large, often unstated share of the work a benchmark score gets attributed to.

Reading the frontier

  • Anthropic’s own price ladder is a clean illustration of tier-vs-capability tradeoffs. Fable 5 ($10/$50) costs more than Opus 5 ($5/$25), which costs more than Sonnet 5 ($3/$15, temporarily $2/$10), which costs more than Haiku 4.5 ($1/$5), a straightforward 10:1 spread top to bottom. Meanwhile the Intelligence Index gap between Fable 5 and Opus 5 is small (60 vs. 61). Opus 5 is plausibly the better cost-adjusted choice for most tasks despite Fable 5’s “most capable” label. artificialanalysis.ai/models
  • xAI’s explicit price-performance claim. Grok 4.5 at $2/$6 per MTok is positioned by xAI as matching GPT-5.5/Codex-class coding performance “at roughly half the per-task cost,” with “2x better token efficiency” claimed generally. Both are vendor claims, not independently verified in this research. techcrunch.com
  • Artificial Analysis’s own cost framing. The site computes a “Cost per Intelligence Index Task” metric and plots an intelligence-vs-cost scatter with a labeled “most attractive quadrant,” the closest thing to a purpose-built intelligence-per-dollar chart in this research, though its current occupants weren’t extractable from the page content fetched here. Check the live chart yourself. artificialanalysis.ai
  • The price spread across the whole market, not just the frontier, is wide. Nova Micro, Sarvam 30B (high), and Gemma 4 E4B are tied at roughly $0.03 per million tokens blended on Artificial Analysis’s view. That’s several orders of magnitude below any frontier model above. artificialanalysis.ai/models See Running models locally for what changes when you own the hardware instead of renting it by the token.
  • A cost variable no benchmark score captures at all. Anthropic’s separate cache-write fee, versus OpenAI’s and DeepSeek’s cache-hit-only pricing, means two models with identical headline per-token prices can have meaningfully different effective costs on a real agentic workload with heavy prompt-caching, the exact pattern a coding agent produces by re-reading the same repo context across many turns. artificialanalysis.ai
  • Effort-level settings change the bill, not just the score. Because reasoning-effort tiers change both the benchmark score and the number of tokens actually billed, “price per million output tokens” understates real cost at higher effort settings: the same nominal rate applies to a much larger realized token count. No source in this research quantified that multiplier directly: flagged here as an open question, not asserted with a number.

Benchmarks are the best public signal available for comparing models you haven’t tried yourself. And every one of them is narrower, staler, or more gameable than its headline number suggests. Read the split. Read the effort tier. Check the date. Then go run the task that actually matters to you, because that’s the only benchmark with a sample size of one that’s guaranteed to be about your work.
