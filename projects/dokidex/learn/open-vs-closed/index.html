:: THE TWO FRONTIERS

Open vs. Closed Weights

Who actually ships open weights versus a locked API, what each licence really permits, and how the best of each side compares on coding, reasoning, and cost, as of July 2026.

Intermediate

“Open” and “closed” sound like two teams. In practice they’re two different business models that both ship you a file of numbers. The file doesn’t tell you which team it came from. What differs is what else you get alongside the weights, what you’re legally allowed to do with them, and how much you pay per token versus per GPU-hour. This page tries to put a number on every one of those differences, dated, so you can make the call for your own situation rather than take either side’s marketing at face value.

Three different promises: open weight, open source, open data

Almost every model people call “open source” in casual conversation is, more precisely, open weight: the publisher hands you the trained parameters and lets you download and run them. That promise is real and valuable. No other permission is required to run, fine-tune, or quantize the model. It’s also, on its own, a much smaller promise than “open source” usually implies for regular software.

The Open Source Initiative’s Open Source AI Definition sets a higher bar. To call an AI system “open source,” it wants three things released with no permission needed to use, study, modify, or share: sufficiently detailed information about the training data (description and provenance, not necessarily the raw data itself), the complete source code used to train and run the system, and the parameters, the “preferred form to make modifications.” Almost nothing in this document clears that bar.

License permissiveness and openness-of-process are independent axes. A model can be Apache-2.0 (maximally permissive to use) while remaining completely closed-process: no data, no training code, just a HuggingFace repo of tensors. That describes nearly every model on this page. Keep the two questions separate: “Can I legally ship a product with this?” is a licence question, answered below. “Can I inspect or reproduce how this was made?” is a process question. For all but one model here, the honest answer is no.

Licences, taken seriously

This is where people get burned: treat every row here as load-bearing. A licence name isn’t decoration, since it determines whether you can put a model in a commercial product at all. Vendors frequently ship different licences for models that look like siblings.

Warning: “Apache-2.0” and “MIT” are genuinely permissive: commercial use, modification, and redistribution with no revenue cap. “Modified MIT,” “Community” licences, and anything with “Research” or “Non-Production” in the name aren’t the same thing, even when the base text reads similarly. Read the actual LICENSE file on the model’s own card before shipping.

Common trap: a per-token price comparison across these vendors is quietly misleading in one specific way. Anthropic’s pricing docs note that Claude 4.7 and later use a tokenizer that produces roughly 30% more tokens for the same text than earlier Claude generations. A “$5 vs $5” sticker-price match against an older Claude price, or against a vendor on a different tokenizer, isn’t apples to apples. The same document costs more real tokens to process on the newer tokenizer. Anthropic also charges a separate cache-write fee on top of input pricing, where OpenAI and DeepSeek typically charge only cache-hit pricing with no write fee, another structural difference no headline number captures.

xAI’s exact current model IDs matter here too: the 4.20 generation ships as three distinct model names rather than one model with a setting: grok-4.20-0309-reasoning, grok-4.20-0309-non-reasoning, and grok-4.20-multi-agent-0309, a different pattern from Anthropic/OpenAI/Google’s single-model effort-dial approach.

Cohere is the interesting exception to “closed frontier.” Its older Command R and Command R+ (cc-by-nc-4.0, above) are effectively closed for production use despite being downloadable. Cohere’s closed, hosted-API-only tier today is the rest of the Command A family (Command A Reasoning, Command A Vision, Command A Translate, Command R7B), for which current per-token pricing wasn’t published on Cohere’s live pricing page at the time of this research (a real gap, not a $0 price). Its new flagship, Command A+, flipped to Apache-2.0 and belongs on the open side of this page (see below).

Marketing tier names don’t always track the independent benchmark. Anthropic’s own docs describe Claude Fable 5 as its “most capable widely released model,” but on the independent Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, plain Claude Opus 5 at max effort scores 61, a point higher than Fable 5’s 60 at matched effort. Fable appears optimized for long-running agent endurance rather than raw single-shot score. Google markets Gemini 3.1 Pro as its flagship reasoning model. On that same index it scores 46, behind Grok 4.5 (54) and well behind the tight Claude/GPT-5.6 cluster at 59-61. Amazon calls Nova “frontier intelligence,” but Nova Premier scores 13, below the roughly-19 median for comparable non-reasoning models, the widest marketing-to-measurement gap in this research. A tier’s name is marketing copy. An independent score is the thing to actually compare.

How they actually compare

Every claim below is labeled vendor-reported (from a lab’s own card or blog) or independent (an evaluator with no stake in the result). The benchmarks page goes much deeper into why the same benchmark name routinely produces wildly different numbers depending on harness, split, and tracker staleness. Read it before trusting any single score too far. This section sticks to the handful of comparisons that held up cleanest.

Coding and agentic tool use

On SWE-bench Verified (a real GitHub issue, a real patch, the repo’s own test suite deciding pass/fail, using a 500-problem hand-filtered set), the independent evaluator vals.ai (dated Jul 22, 2026) put the frontier within a tight band: Claude Opus 5 97.0%, GPT-5.6 Sol 96.2%, Claude Fable 5 95.0%, then Kimi K3 at 93.4%, GPT-5.6 Luna 93.0%, Claude Opus 4.8 88.6%, Grok 4.5 86.6%, and Gemini 3.1 Pro at 80.6% (a vendor-reported single-attempt figure from Google’s own card). The benchmark is close to saturated at the top. Those numbers don’t discriminate much between the leaders anymore.

The harder SWE-bench Pro illustrates why a bare score is often meaningless: depending on which split you read, the same model generation scores anywhere from the high 40s to the low 80s. Scale AI’s standardized public split puts GPT-5.4 at 59.1%, while its harder private/commercial split scores Claude Opus 4.6 at just 47.1%. A different tracker (benchlm.ai) reports Claude Opus 5 at 79.2% on what’s likely the easier public split. These aren’t disagreements about the same thing. They’re different measurement conditions sharing one benchmark name.

On the vendor side: Alibaba’s own card for Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B describes results “comparable to Claude Sonnet” on agentic coding and browser-use, a vendor comparison, stated as such. Zhipu’s own comparison for GLM is more candid: it explicitly admits GLM-4.6 still lags Claude Sonnet 4.5 on coding, a rare instance of a vendor citing its own shortfall directly. Cohere reports its Command A+ jumping from 3% to 25% on Terminal-Bench Hard agentic coding versus its prior model, vendor-reported, and it’s worth noting that even the improved number is modest in absolute terms.

On agentic tool use specifically, Artificial Analysis’s composite index has a dedicated sub-score: Grok 4.5 is reported #1 on that one component even though it ranks well behind the leaders overall (54 vs. 59-61), evidence that a single overall “smartest model” number can hide real category-specific strengths.

Math and reasoning

AIME 2025 is fully saturated: on llm-stats.com’s tracker, five models hit a perfect 1.000: Grok-4 Heavy, GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Pro, GPT-5.2 Pro, and the open-weight Kimi K2-Thinking-0905, with Claude Opus 4.6 at 0.998. A perfect score on a fixed, public contest paper says more about that paper being exhausted than about remaining headroom.

GPQA Diamond (198 PhD-vetted questions across biology, physics, chemistry, the hardest subset of the 448-question full GPQA set) is close behind: GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Mythos Preview tie at 94.6%, against a human PhD baseline of 65-74%. Frontier models now score above the expert baseline, which raises the question of whether the remaining gains reflect better reasoning or exploitation of the dataset’s known answer-key errors. Every number in this specific tracker is vendor self-reported. None are independently re-run.

Harder math benchmarks (FrontierMath, Humanity’s Last Exam) have far messier, contested numbers across sources right now (one tracker’s own leaderboard table directly contradicts its own descriptive prose). Rather than repeat a number likely to be stale or mismeasured, the benchmarks page walks through exactly why, and what to trust instead.

Long context

Headline context windows and reliable use of that window are two different claims. A growing body of independent research says so directly. Chroma Research’s “Context Rot” study ran 18 models across Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and Alibaba and found performance degrades non-uniformly well before the model’s stated context limit, that semantically-similar (not just lexically exact) needle/question pairs make it much worse than the classic test suggests, that a single topically-related distractor hurts meaningfully, and, counterintuitively, that shuffling a coherent document into randomized sentences sometimes improved retrieval. On a synthetic repeat-this-sequence task, models began drifting into unrelated words starting around 500-750 tokens, far below any advertised window. Claude models tended to abstain (“I don’t have that information”) even when the answer was present. GPT models hallucinated instead. Both failure modes worsen with length. (trychroma.com/research/context-rot)

Against that backdrop, Nemotron 3 (all three tiers), MiniMax-M1/M3, DeepSeek-V4, and GLM-5.2 all claim native or extended 1M-token context. Llama 4 Scout’s marketed 10M-token figure is the single largest number in this whole landscape. But no independent long-context recall evaluation of Scout at that length was found. Treat it as an architectural ceiling, not a demonstrated usable length. Frontier labs have started shipping harder successor tests instead of plain needle-in-a-haystack: Google reports Gemini 3.1 Pro at 84.9% on MRCR v2, a test that requires tracking references across a long multi-turn conversation rather than retrieving one lexically distinctive fact, a meaningfully harder and more honest test of what long context is actually for.

Multilingual

Meta’s own blog claims Llama 4 pretrained on 200 languages, over 100 with more than a billion tokens each: “10x more multilingual tokens than Llama 3,” a vendor claim. Google’s Gemma 3 supports 140+ languages, while TII’s Falcon-H1 natively supports 18 languages, scalable to 100+. Cohere’s Command A+ rounds out the group at 48 languages, consistent with Cohere’s long-standing enterprise/translation focus (the company also ships a dedicated Command A Translate model).

Vision

Alibaba’s Qwen3-VL architecture supports native 256K interleaved text/image/video context across both dense and MoE tiers. AI2’s Molmo line is built specifically around pointing and grounding: identifying exact pixel locations for objects, not just describing images in prose. Pointing and grounding like that is a genuinely distinguishing capability against most vision-language models. On MMMU-Pro: Cohere reports Command A+ at 63% while Google reports Gemini 3.1 Pro at 80.5%, both vendor-reported, each on its own harness. Two vendor-reported scores on two different harnesses hit the same trap flagged above with SWE-bench Pro: a shared benchmark name isn’t a shared measurement condition. So this isn’t a legitimate direct closed-vs-open comparison either, however clean the two numbers look side by side.

The gap, quantified and dated

Two honest readings exist. They say different things. Don’t average them or pick whichever one flatters the side you already prefer.

Current snapshot (Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.1, July 2026): the highest-scoring open-weight model is GLM-5.2 at max effort, 51. The highest-scoring model overall is Claude Opus 5 at max effort, 61, a 10-point gap on this one aggregate benchmark, independently measured rather than vendor self-reported. Within “open” there’s a wide spread too: NVIDIA’s Nemotron 3 Ultra is vendor-cited around 48 (not independently re-verified), MiniMax-M3 scores 44, and Cohere’s Command A+ scores 37. So GLM-5.2 leading its own field by a wide margin is itself informative.

Historical reading (Epoch AI, an analysis from the Llama-3.1 era, i.e. well before this snapshot): the best open models lagged the best closed models by roughly one year, a 5-to-22-month lag across specific benchmarks (MMLU, GPQA, GSM1k, BBH) and about a 15-month training-compute lag for the largest models. Meta’s Llama 3.1 405B was cited as closing the compute gap with GPT-4 roughly 16 months after GPT-4 shipped. The same analysis found a real nuance: looking only at each side’s single best model, training compute grew at essentially the same rate on both sides: 4.6×/year for open and 4.6×/year for closed, with that ~15-month lag holding steady. So the top-1 gap reads as roughly constant, not narrowing. But looking at the top 10 models on each side, closed labs were scaling training compute faster (5.0×/year) than open releases (3.6×/year), i.e. a widening lag once you look past the single leader.

Why the two readings differ: these aren’t measuring the same thing. The 2026 snapshot is a single-index, single-leader comparison at one moment. The Epoch analysis is a multi-benchmark, whole-ecosystem comparison over time. Put together: the gap between the single best open model and the single best closed model appears to have compressed since 2024-2025: 10 points on one index reads smaller than a documented ~1-year, multi-benchmark lag. But the broader open ecosystem, beyond that one leading model, may still be falling further behind. No source found this research connects those two readings into one continuous line. So neither should be quoted as if it settles the question alone.

Renting the best open model, instead of running it

An awkward gap sits between the two halves of this page: GLM-5.2 is the strongest open-weight model measured here. At 753B parameters, essentially nobody is running it at home. Open weights answer “may I”, not “can I afford the hardware”.

Z.ai sells the middle option: API access to its own open-weight models, priced as a subscription rather than per token. The GLM Coding Plan starts at $18/month for a Lite tier, with Pro and Max above it, and works by pointing an existing agent at an Anthropic-compatible endpoint rather than shipping its own client (overview, setup).

  Lite Pro Max
Prompts per 5-hour window ~80 ~400 ~1,600
Prompts per week ~400 ~2,000 ~8,000

GLM-5.2 itself is documented at a 1M-token context with 128K max output, and exposes a reasoning_effort parameter for trading latency against depth, the same dial Anthropic and OpenAI now expose, and a sign of how quickly that control became table stakes (model docs). Z.ai’s own comparisons against GLM-5.1 are vendor-reported and should be read as such.

The quota unit is a prompt, not a token. Z.ai states one prompt triggers roughly 15-20 model invocations, an agentic turn, not a chat message. It puts the plans at “approximately 15-30× the monthly subscription fee” in equivalent API value. GLM-5.2 and GLM-5-Turbo draw 3× quota during peak hours (14:00-18:00 UTC+8) and 2× off-peak. Plans include GLM-5.2, GLM-5-Turbo and GLM-4.7.

Configuration is three environment variables in ~/.claude/settings.json: an auth token, a base URL of https://api.z.ai/api/anthropic, and a raised timeout, after which Claude Code, Cline or OpenCode talks to GLM instead of its native models.

Note: this is worth understanding as a category, not just a product. Because Claude Code reads its endpoint from configuration, any provider exposing an Anthropic-compatible API can host it. Several now do. The agent and the model behind it have come apart: a tool’s name no longer tells you whose weights answered. That’s good for switching costs and bad for reasoning about where your code went. Check the base URL before assuming.

What it’s not: a route to running GLM-5.2 yourself. You’re renting inference from Z.ai under its terms and privacy policy, on the same trust footing as any other API. The weights being MIT-licensed means you could self-host given roughly 400GB of accelerator memory at 4-bit. It doesn’t mean this subscription is local. For what actually runs on one consumer GPU, see Running models locally.

What each side is genuinely good at

Closed wins on: the absolute reasoning ceiling, day-one access to whatever is newest, the deepest agentic-coding and tool-use track record, and the broadest first-party tooling. That last one covers realtime voice, image and video generation, and enterprise SLAs no self-hosted stack can offer.

Two of those deserve their numbers. The reasoning ceiling is a 10-point gap on the current best-vs-best index, or a 5-to-22-month historical time lag by the other reading, and the two don’t average into one figure. On the coding record, SWE-bench Verified’s top three are all closed models clustered in the mid-90s%. The fourth-place model, Kimi K3 at 93.4%, has weights that are announced but not yet released, per Moonshot’s own release-countdown page dated 2026-07-27. So the best score below that closed top three currently belongs to a model nobody can actually run.

Open wins on: zero marginal cost once you own the hardware, complete data privacy (nothing leaves the machine, by construction, not a policy promise), no rate limits and no deprecation notice retiring the model a workflow was built around, full control to modify, quantize, fine-tune, or prune, and genuine competitiveness at coding specifically: Qwen3-Coder, GLM, Kimi, and DeepSeek all sit within the top tier on SWE-bench Verified even without leading it. Some open models are also directly rentable far below frontier-closed prices: MiniMax-M3’s own hosted API, for instance, runs $0.30/$1.20 per MTok for inputs under 512K (a fraction of any Fable-tier closed price) for anyone who wants the licence flexibility of open weights without buying a GPU.

Where local/open genuinely loses: the reasoning ceiling on the hardest problems, full stop. Nobody should pretend a 30B model on a consumer card matches a frontier reasoning model on novel, research-level tasks. Most of the current open frontier (everything at 100B+ total parameters, which by mid-2026 is most of the field) needs serious multi-GPU hardware or a hosted provider, not a single gaming GPU. The local inference page puts exact VRAM numbers on that. Long-context reliability is a shared weakness (see Context Rot, above) rather than an open-specific one. But closed labs currently publish more evidence of working around it (MRCR-style harder tests) than most open labs do. And there’s no self-hosted equivalent of an enterprise SLA: if the model misbehaves at 3am, that’s your on-call rotation, not a vendor’s.

  • 10 ptsGap between the single best open and single best closed model, one index, Jul 2026
  • $0.03Cheapest blended price per MTok on Artificial Analysis, a three-way tie: Nova Micro, Sarvam 30B, and the open-weight Gemma 4 E4B
  • ~30%More tokens Claude 4.7+ produces for the same text vs. earlier tokenizers, which breaks naive price comparisons

The practical takeaway: cost, privacy, latency, and control are the axes where open genuinely wins outright, not just “wins for now until closed gets cheap too.” The reasoning ceiling is the axis where closed genuinely wins, and probably will keep winning at the frontier for a while. The compute and data budgets involved in training the very largest closed models aren’t something a consumer GPU stack replicates. Pick the side that matches which axis actually matters for the task in front of you and revisit the pick when the numbers on this page are inevitably stale. Check the dates.
