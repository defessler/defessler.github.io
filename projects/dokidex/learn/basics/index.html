Foundations

What a model actually is, why tokens aren't words, why the context window isn't memory, and why models hallucinate: the mechanics behind every chatbot, explained from zero.

Beginner

This page assumes nothing except that you’ve typed a message to a chatbot at some point. No math beyond arithmetic, no code. By the end you should be able to explain, correctly, what happens between you pressing Enter and text appearing on the screen, and why several of the things most people believe about that process are wrong in specific, useful-to-know ways.

Each section below gives you the correct one-line definition, the misconception it replaces, and an analogy, including where the analogy stops being accurate. That last part matters as much as the analogy itself: a metaphor that’s wrong only 10% of the time is exactly the 10% that gets people in trouble later.

What a model actually is

A trained model is a large set of fixed numbers (weights) that turns an input into an output through repeated arithmetic, learned from data rather than written by a programmer. Nothing more mysterious than that sits underneath. There’s no sentence-retrieval engine, no indexed archive of everything it read.

Common trap: “The model looks things up in a database of things it was trained on.” There’s no retrievable copy of the training text sitting inside the weights. Nor is a model some kind of compressed search index. It typically can’t reproduce most of what it was trained on word-for-word.

The best analogy: a model is like a student who read millions of books, closed every one of them, and now answers from a compressed sense of the patterns those books contained, not by flipping back to a remembered page. Where this breaks: a student can say “I don’t actually remember that one,” a calibrated admission of uncertainty spoken out loud. A model does compute a probability for every token it produces, and a pretrained model’s raw probabilities tend to be reasonably well calibrated. But it has no reliably-verbalized, separately reported confidence signal. The later RLHF fine-tuning stage that turns it into a helpful chatbot is well documented to degrade that calibration further. That gap between what the model computes and what it actually says is exactly what causes hallucination, covered later on this page.

Training vs. inference: two different activities

Training is the rare, expensive process of adjusting weights against a huge dataset. Inference is the constant, cheap process of running those now-fixed weights on your specific input. These are different jobs, running at different times, usually on different hardware, and confusing them is where a lot of chatbot folklore comes from.

Common trap: “The model keeps learning from every conversation I have with it.” Standard deployed models don’t update their weights from your chats in real time. The weights are frozen the moment training finishes. What feels like “picking up on” something mid-conversation is the model conditioning on the growing text in front of it, not a weight change.

Training is like carving a statue over months. Inference is the finished statue standing in a museum, reacting to the light in the room (your prompt) without re-carving itself every time someone walks by. The metaphor has a limit, though: the company behind the model does periodically go back and carve a new statue (a new model version) after a fresh training run. The statue itself never changes. The museum occasionally swaps it for a newer one.

Tokens: why “word” is the wrong unit

Before a model ever sees your text, a tokenizer splits it into tokens, sub-word chunks produced by an algorithm (commonly byte-pair encoding, BPE) that merges the most frequent adjacent symbol pairs in its training corpus until it hits a fixed vocabulary size. Byte-pair encoding A token can be a whole common word, a fragment of a rarer one, a single character, or a punctuation mark, whichever chunk the merge process happened to settle on.

Common trap: “A token is a word,” or “a token is some fixed unit like a syllable.” Neither is right. GPT-3.5/GPT-4-era tokenizers use vocabularies on the order of 100,000 entries and build each token by applying their learned merge rules (in the order those rules were learned) repeatedly, starting from raw bytes, until no learned pair remains to merge. That’s greedy over merge rank, not over span length, which is why the same text can tokenize to a different count of tokens under a different tokenizer version. Byte-pair encoding Anthropic notes that its Claude Opus 4.7-and-later tokenizer generation produces roughly 30% more tokens than earlier tokenizers for identical text (a vendor-reported figure, as of July 2026). Claude models overview

Tokenization is like a printer’s type case: it has pre-cast pieces for common whole words (“the”, “ing”) but falls back to spelling rare words letter-by-letter from individual character pieces. Here’s the edge of the metaphor: each type-case piece has one fixed meaning by design. A token’s meaning to the model depends entirely on the surrounding tokens instead. It’s the model, not the tokenizer, that supplies context. Byte-level BPE (used since GPT-2) also operates on raw bytes rather than human-legible characters, which means a chunk boundary can land mid-character for non-Latin scripts. Byte-pair encoding

This is also a major reason “how many R’s are in strawberry”-style questions trip models up. Part of it is that the word typically arrives as just a handful of token chunks (the exact split is tokenizer-dependent: three chunks is common in GPT-4-era tokenizers), never as individual letters, so asking it to count letters asks it to introspect on a decomposition it was never given at the input stage. But that’s not the whole story: models can spell a word out letter-by-letter on request and still miscount, even with the letters already separated. So tokenization makes the task needlessly hard without being the entire explanation. Byte-pair encoding; Hugging Face LLM course, BPE

Embeddings: meaning as geometry

An embedding is a vector (a list of numbers, hundreds to thousands of entries long) assigned to a piece of text such that distance and direction in that space reflect learned relationships between meanings. Nothing is hand-labeled. The geometry emerges from training.

Common trap: “Embeddings just group synonyms together,” or “an embedding literally encodes a dictionary definition.” What embeddings actually capture is distributional and relational structure. The canonical demonstration is vector arithmetic: vector(“king”) − vector(“man”) + vector(“woman”) lands close to vector(“queen”), a relationship, not a definition. That demonstration also depends on excluding “king,” “man,” and “woman” themselves from the nearest-neighbor search. Without that exclusion, the nearest vector to the arithmetic is usually “king” again. King − Man + Woman = Queen; Linzen, 2016; Nissim et al., 2020

Think of embeddings as GPS coordinates for meaning: the step from “France” to “Paris” points in the same direction, and roughly the same distance, as the step from “Japan” to “Tokyo”, without anyone hand-labelling a “capital-of” direction. Where this breaks, badly if taken literally: GPS coordinates are two numbers on a fixed map. Embedding space has hundreds or thousands of dimensions, most of which correspond to nothing a human can name. Word2Vec Explained And the king/queen example itself comes from an older, static style of embedding (word2vec). In a modern transformer, a token’s position in meaning-space shifts depending on the surrounding context. “Bank” near a river and “bank” near a loan application aren’t fixed at the same coordinates at all.

Next-token prediction: the whole engine

An autoregressive language model is trained to output a probability distribution over its entire vocabulary for “what token comes next,” given everything before it. Text is generated by repeatedly sampling one token from that distribution and appending it to the input. That loop, run one token at a time, is the entire generative mechanism. There’s no separate module underneath it doing something else.

Common trap: “The model plans out the whole sentence, or the whole answer, in advance, then writes it.” There’s no designed look-ahead module: the mechanism computes one distribution, samples one token, and feeds that choice back in to compute the next distribution, over and over. That doesn’t mean nothing resembling planning ever happens inside a single one of those steps: Anthropic’s interpretability research found Claude activating candidate rhyming words several tokens before writing the line that needed them when composing poetry. Any such planning is an emergent property of training, not a separate architectural feature. On the Biology of a Large Language Model, Anthropic 2025

It’s like an extremely well-read person playing an extended game of “finish the sentence,” one word at a time, each guess conditioned on every word so far. The metaphor stops short here, though: a person playing that game can consciously narrate a plan to themselves before speaking. The base mechanism has no designed planning phase like that. Any foresight it exhibits is a side effect learned during training, surfacing inside the same single forward pass that produces the next token, not a separate step before it. Reasoning-trained models add explicit intermediate “thinking” tokens that function like scratch work, which is a real but separate technique covered on the next page.

The context window: not memory

The context window is the maximum number of tokens (input and output combined, in most APIs) that a model can attend to in a single forward pass. Anything beyond that limit never reaches the model at all unless an application chunks or summarizes it first. As of July 2026, Anthropic’s current top-tier models (Claude Opus 5, Claude Sonnet 5, and Claude Fable 5) ship a 1,000,000 token context window with up to 128,000 output tokens, while Claude Haiku 4.5 ships a smaller 200,000 token window with up to 64,000 output tokens (vendor-reported figures). Claude models overview

Common trap: “The context window is like long-term memory: the model remembers our past conversations.” A context window is only the text physically present in the current request. Nothing persists between separate calls unless the surrounding application explicitly re-sends prior text (or logs it to an external store) every single time.

The context window is like the surface of a desk you can spread papers on while working: anything not currently on the desk is gone from view, even if it’s in a filing cabinet (the training data) or was on the desk five minutes ago (an earlier turn that scrolled out of the window). One place the desk analogy strains: unlike a desk, a bigger context window isn’t “free” comprehension. Independent research found a well-documented U-shaped performance drop for information placed in the middle of a long context compared with the start or end. A large window doesn’t guarantee the model uses everything in it equally well. Lost in the Middle (Liu et al., TACL 2023)

Temperature and sampling

Temperature is a number that rescales the model’s output probabilities before a token is sampled: low temperature sharpens the distribution toward the most likely token, high temperature flattens it toward more uniform, more random selection. Generation configurations: temperature, top-k, top-p It changes how a token gets picked from a distribution the model already computed, nothing more.

Common trap: “Temperature 0 means the model is being more factual or careful,” or the opposite, “high temperature makes the model smarter or more creative in a meaningful way.” Temperature has no mechanism for checking truth. At temperature 0 the model deterministically outputs its single most-likely token. And that token can be confidently wrong. Temperature governs sampling randomness, not factual calibration: there’s no step anywhere in it that consults whether the chosen token is true. It doesn’t follow that error rates are identical at every setting (sampling further down the distribution does measurably change how often a model goes wrong), only that turning temperature down buys you consistency, not correctness.

Temperature is a dial on how much you round toward the “safe favorite” versus roll dice among plausible options, like recommending the top restaurant every time (low temperature) versus picking randomly from a weighted list of the ten best (higher temperature). Where this breaks: “rolling dice among plausible options” implies every sampled option is reasonable, but raw temperature alone can still surface very unlikely, nonsensical tokens from the distribution’s long tail. That’s why nucleus (top-p) sampling is usually layered on top. It first truncates the candidate set to the smallest group whose combined probability exceeds a threshold (commonly 0.9 to 0.95) before sampling from that narrowed set. Top-p sampling, Wikipedia

Why models hallucinate

Hallucination is confident generation of false or unsupported content, and recent theoretical work argues it’s a predictable consequence of training and evaluation incentives rather than a mysterious glitch. The formal argument reduces text generation to a kind of classification problem (“is this candidate output valid or not”) and shows generation error is bounded below by error on that same classification task. Why Language Models Hallucinate (Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, Zhang), arXiv:2509.04664

Common trap: “Hallucination is a bug that better training data or a bigger model will simply fix.” The paper’s central argument is that standard evaluation actively rewards guessing over expressing uncertainty. Most benchmarks score a confident wrong answer exactly the same as an honest “I’m not sure,” both zero credit. A model optimized against those benchmarks learns to bluff rather than abstain instead of admitting doubt. The proposed fix is reworking evaluation incentives, not simply scaling up. Why Language Models Hallucinate, OpenAI

A model forced to always answer is like a student penalized equally for “I don’t know” and a wrong guess on a multiple-choice test: the rational test-taking strategy is to always guess, never abstain, which is exactly the behavior that looks like hallucination once the stakes are open-ended factual claims instead of exam bubbles. But the comparison only goes so far: a human test-taker consciously chooses to guess. A model has no single internal “true belief” being suppressed. There’s no fact-checker being overridden. The hallucinated sentence and the correct sentence are produced by the identical mechanism described two sections up (next-token prediction). The model does compute a probability for every token, and a pretrained model’s raw probabilities tend to be reasonably well calibrated. But training gives it no reliable way to report that difference out loud, and RLHF, the stage that makes it conversational, is documented to degrade that calibration further.

Prompting basics that follow from the mechanism

A prompt is the entire text input given to the model at inference time (instructions, context, examples, and the actual question), and the model has no separate settings beyond what’s expressed in that text plus a handful of API parameters like temperature. Everything above on this page converges here: no memory (so state your context every time), no planning ahead (so ambiguity gets resolved by the model’s best local guess, not a clarifying question), and a next-token mechanism that conditions entirely on what’s literally in front of it.

Common trap: “There’s a single secret phrase or trick prompt that unlocks hidden capability.” Most reliable prompting gains come from unglamorous specificity: stating the desired format, giving concrete examples, separating instructions from data, and being explicit about constraints, not a magic incantation.

Prompting is like giving instructions to a very capable new contractor who has read everything but knows nothing about your specific job. Vague instructions get generically plausible but often wrong-for-you results. A clear brief with examples and constraints gets a targeted one. Where this breaks: a human contractor asks clarifying questions when instructions are ambiguous, and remembers your preferences on the next job. A stateless model will confidently fill ambiguity with its own assumption instead of asking (unless you explicitly prompt it to ask). It remembers nothing next session, for exactly the reason covered in the context-window section above.

Three habits fall directly out of the mechanics on this page, not out of folklore:

  • Be explicit, because there’s no clarifying question by default. The model fills gaps with its own best guess rather than pausing to ask: say what you want the way you’d write it down for someone who can’t follow up.
  • Show examples, because the model is completing a pattern. Next-token prediction means it’s extending whatever shape you’ve given it. Give it the shape you want extended.
  • Repeat what matters across turns, because nothing persists automatically. If a constraint from three turns ago still applies, say so. It fell out of view exactly like anything else that scrolled past the edge of the context window.

FAQ

Is the model “thinking,” or just predicting the next word? Mechanically, the base process is next-token prediction: given everything so far, the model computes a probability distribution over possible next tokens and samples one, repeatedly. Reasoning-trained models add an extra step (generating explicit intermediate tokens before the final answer, which measurably helps on complex tasks). But that’s a distinct, later-stage technique covered on the next page, not a change to the base mechanism. Chain-of-Thought Prompting, arXiv:2201.11903

Does the model remember me between chats? Not by default. Each request is self-contained. Unless an application explicitly stores and re-sends prior conversation text, or uses a dedicated memory feature, nothing persists: the model’s weights don’t change from talking to you, and no session state is kept between separate calls.

Why does the model sometimes make things up so confidently? Because standard training and evaluation reward confident answers and score a wrong guess the same as an honest “I’m not sure” — both get zero credit — so the strategy that wins is to never abstain. Why Language Models Hallucinate, arXiv:2509.04664

If I set temperature to 0, will the model stop making mistakes? No. Temperature 0 just means the model always picks its single highest-probability next token instead of sampling — if that top token happens to be wrong, it will confidently and consistently produce the wrong answer. Temperature controls randomness in token choice, not factual accuracy. Generation configurations

Why can’t the model reliably count the letters in a word? Because it typically never sees individual letters as separate units: a word is usually broken into just a few sub-word tokens before the model processes it, so a letter-counting question asks it to reason about a decomposition it was never given. Tokenization is a major reason this is needlessly hard, but it isn’t the whole story: models can still miscount even when asked to spell a word out letter-by-letter first. Byte-pair encoding

What’s actually different between a “base model” and the assistant I chat with? A base, pretrained-only model just continues whatever pattern is in front of it: given an instruction, it’s just as likely to continue with more instructions as to follow the first one. The assistant you chat with went through additional training stages specifically aimed at following instructions reliably and matching preferred style. That training reshapes behavior and helpfulness far more than it adds new factual knowledge. The mechanics of exactly how are covered on the next page.

Why do bigger context windows sometimes not help as much as expected? Because supporting a longer window (a technical limit) and reliably using everything inside it are two separate problems. Independent measurement found a strong U-shaped pattern: notably worse recall for information placed in the middle of a long context than at the very start or end. Bigger doesn’t automatically mean evenly used. Lost in the Middle, arXiv:2307.03172

Why does the same prompt sometimes use a different number of tokens on different models? Because tokenization is model- and version-specific: different tokenizer vocabularies split the same text differently. Anthropic notes its Claude Opus 4.7-and-later tokenizer generation produces roughly 30% more tokens than the prior generation for identical text, as of July 2026: a tokenizer change, not a change in the text itself. Claude models overview

That’s the whole surface-level mechanism: weights, tokens, a probability distribution, and a loop. Everything past this point (attention, the transformer block, how training actually happens in stages, tool use, retrieval) is built directly on top of these nine ideas.
