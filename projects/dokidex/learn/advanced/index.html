Advanced: MoE, Attention Variants, Quantization, and the Research Frontier

How production LLMs are actually built and served: mixture-of-experts routing, the attention variants that shrink the KV cache, quantization theory, speculative decoding, scaling laws, RL post-training, and the questions that are still genuinely open.

Advanced

This tier assumes you already have the transformer architecture and self-attention from How models work: Query/Key/Value, multi-head attention, residual stacks, KV caches. What follows is the layer above that: the systems and research choices that turn “a transformer” into a model you can train affordably and serve at scale, plus an honest look at what’s still unsettled. None of this is decoration. Our own VRAM and throughput math, elsewhere on this site, rests on getting these ideas right.

Mixture of Experts: routing, not specialization

A dense transformer’s feed-forward sub-layer runs the same weights on every token. A Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer replaces that single feed-forward block with many smaller expert feed-forward blocks, plus a learned router that picks a small subset (two of eight in Mixtral, and as few as four or eight out of 128 to 256 in the fine-grained designs that followed) to actually run for each token. Mixtral of Experts (Jiang et al.), arXiv:2401.04088 That’s what lets a model’s total parameter count run into the hundreds of billions while its active parameter count (what actually does compute for any given token) stays a small fraction of that.

It’s tempting to picture each expert as a specialist (“the math expert,” “the code expert”), but that’s not what’s happening. Routing is a learned, per-token, per-layer decision based on that token’s representation at that specific point in the network, not a document-level or topic-level classification. There’s no evidence experts map cleanly onto human categories at all. Mixtral of Experts, arXiv:2401.04088 Because routing decisions are made independently per token and per layer, usage doesn’t naturally spread evenly across experts: left alone, a router tends to pile traffic onto a few popular ones, starving the rest of training signal. Production MoE training corrects for this with explicit load-balancing machinery: an auxiliary loss term penalizing uneven usage, or (DeepSeek-V3’s refinement) a bias-based routing adjustment that achieves balance without an auxiliary loss diluting the main training objective. DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report, arXiv:2412.19437

Here is the point this site’s numbers depend on:

Key idea: MoE saves compute, not memory. The router picks a different expert combination for every token, and over a real request that sweeps across most or all experts. So every one of them has to be resident in VRAM (or fast storage) before you can serve a single token, whether or not any given token touches a given expert. A model described as “XB total / YB active” needs memory sized to X, not Y. DeepSeek-V3 ships 671B total parameters with roughly 37B active per token, but running it means holding the full 671B in memory. Wikipedia: DeepSeek The pattern holds at consumer scale too: Gemma 4’s 26B-A4B is 25.2B total parameters with about 3.8B active. google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it model card Active parameters tell you how fast it runs. Total parameters tell you whether it fits.

This is exactly the distinction Running models locally uses to size a VRAM budget: check total parameters against your memory first, and only then look at active parameters to estimate speed.

GQA, MQA, and MLA: shrinking the KV cache, not the FLOPs

The KV cache (the stored Key and Value vectors for every previous token, at every layer) is what makes autoregressive generation linear instead of quadratic per step. It also grows with every token generated. Reading it back on every step is a real memory-bandwidth cost. Three schemes attack that cost directly:

  • Multi-Query Attention (MQA): every query head shares one single key/value head.
  • Grouped-Query Attention (GQA): the middle ground: query heads are grouped, and each group shares one key/value head. GQA (Ainslie et al.), arXiv:2305.13245
  • Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA): instead of sharing, keys and values are low-rank compressed into a single small latent vector that gets cached and decompressed back up on demand. DeepSeek-V2, arXiv:2405.04434

Common trap: none of these make the attention computation itself cheaper. Their target is the KV cache’s memory footprint and the bandwidth cost of streaming it back on every decoding step, which, on modern accelerators, is very often the actual bottleneck, not the matrix-multiply FLOPs. GQA, arXiv:2305.13245; DeepSeek-V2, arXiv:2405.04434

The trade-off isn’t as linear as “more sharing, more quality loss” implies. GQA’s own paper reports converting an already-trained multi-head checkpoint to GQA via “uptraining” using only about 5% of its original pretraining compute, recovering quality close to full multi-head attention while keeping speed close to MQA. GQA, arXiv:2305.13245 MLA goes further, reporting quality that exceeds GQA at an even smaller cache footprint, not simply a lesser evil, but a better point on the curve. DeepSeek-V2, arXiv:2405.04434

Quantization: what’s actually being approximated

Quantization stores weights (and sometimes activations or the KV cache) using fewer bits per number (4-bit integers instead of 16-bit floats is the common case), shrinking memory footprint and often raising throughput, at some cost to precision. LLM Quantization Guide Rather than quantizing a whole tensor with one scale, practical schemes quantize in small blocks: each block of weights (say, 32 or 64 values) gets its own scale factor, stored as small integers relative to that scale. Block-wise scaling matters because weight magnitudes vary a lot across a tensor: one global scale would waste precision on small values and clip the large ones.

Misconception: “quantizing to N fewer bits degrades quality roughly proportionally across all weights.” AWQ’s central finding is that weight importance is highly uneven: protecting roughly the top 1% of activation-salient weight channels (found by looking at activation magnitude, not weight magnitude) captures most of the achievable error reduction. Naive uniform rounding spends precision where it doesn’t matter and can damage the small number of channels that matter disproportionately. AWQ (Lin et al.), arXiv:2306.00978

GGUF’s “K-quant” formats (Q4_K, Q5_K, and similar) are what llama.cpp uses, portable across CPU and GPU. They take a coarser, static version of this idea rather than falling back to naive uniform round-to-nearest: super-block scale/min quantization, combined with a hand-picked rule about which tensor types get more bits (Q4_K_M, for instance, bumps attention.wv and feed_forward.w2 up to Q6_K). GPTQ/AWQ/GGUF practitioner overview That rule is a real, empirically-earned instinct (those tensor types are reliably more error-sensitive), but it’s fixed at build time by tensor type, not derived from any calibration data. llama.cpp’s actual analog to AWQ’s insight is the importance matrix (imatrix): activation-derived salience data from running a calibration set through the model. The I-quant formats (IQ2_XS, IQ3_XXS, and similar) use it to weight which values matter most, the same channel-level, data-driven idea AWQ uses, as against K-quants’ static, tensor-type-level rule. Quality genuinely holds up well under moderate, importance-aware compression. It falls off where too few distinct code points remain to represent a salient channel’s range at all, a property of the scheme running out of room, not evidence that quantization is a fixed, linear quality tax.

Speculative decoding: a latency win, not a quality trade

A small, fast draft model proposes several tokens ahead. The large target model checks all of them in one parallel forward pass, accepting the prefix matching what it would have produced on its own and rejecting (and correcting) the rest. The verification step is built so the final accepted output distribution is identical to running the target model alone: the paper reports a 2x to 3x speedup on T5-XXL with no change to output quality. Leviathan et al., arXiv:2211.17192 That distributional guarantee is real, but “free” isn’t: the draft model still runs a forward pass every round, and the target verifies tokens it may end up discarding, so speculative decoding increases total FLOPs spent and needs enough spare VRAM to hold a second model. What it buys is single-stream latency (fewer sequential decode steps on the critical path), not free compute. In large-batch, compute-bound serving, where the GPU is already saturated with useful work across many concurrent requests, the draft model’s extra FLOPs compete for the same compute instead of using idle capacity, and throughput can fall rather than improve.

Misconception: “a weaker draft model makes the final answer worse.” A bad draft guess is simply rejected and regenerated by the target model. It never gets a chance to water down the result. The draft model’s only job is to guess fast. Correctness is entirely the target model’s, exactly as if speculative decoding weren’t in the loop at all.

Flash attention: the bottleneck is moving data, not doing math

FlashAttention is an exact algorithm for computing self-attention (not an approximation) that reorders the computation to minimize traffic between a GPU’s slow high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and its fast on-chip SRAM, using tiling and a fused, block-wise softmax instead of materializing the full attention matrix in HBM. FlashAttention (Dao et al.), arXiv:2205.14135 The paper explicitly contrasts itself with prior approximate attention methods that traded quality for speed by changing what gets computed: FlashAttention changes only how it’s computed, reporting measured speedups (roughly 15% on BERT-large, 3x on GPT-2) with zero change to the result. FlashAttention, arXiv:2205.14135

This is the same underlying story as GQA and MLA, from a different angle. GQA/MLA shrink what has to be stored and re-read across decoding steps. FlashAttention shrinks what has to move between memory tiers within a single attention computation. Both exist because, on current accelerators, moving data is very often the real constraint on serving speed. Raw arithmetic throughput is comparatively cheap. One sentence to keep: modern LLM inference is usually memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound, and a lot of “efficiency” research is really bandwidth-reduction research under a different name.

Scaling laws: Chinchilla, and why the field kept going past it

Scaling laws are empirically fit power-law relationships between a model’s loss and the resources spent training it (parameters, tokens, compute), letting researchers predict a larger run’s payoff before spending the money on it. Scaling Laws for LLMs The most consequential correction to the original (2020) scaling laws came from Hoffmann et al.’s Chinchilla work: existing large models, like the 280B-parameter Gopher, were significantly undertrained relative to their size. A 70B-parameter model (Chinchilla) trained on 1.4 trillion tokens outperformed the far larger Gopher at the same total training compute: for a fixed compute budget, model size and training-token count should scale together, not model size alone. Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al.), arXiv:2203.15556

Common trap: “scaling laws say bigger is always better.” Chinchilla’s whole result is that parameter count in isolation is a misleading quality proxy. A smaller model with proportionally more training tokens can beat a larger, undertrained one at identical compute cost.

Chinchilla’s ratio is itself scoped to one objective: minimizing loss for a fixed training compute budget. It says nothing about what a model costs after training, once it’s deployed and queried possibly billions of times. A model trains once and serves continuously, so if inference cost dominates a model’s lifetime cost (the usual case at real scale), the economically rational choice can be a model smaller than the Chinchilla-optimal point for its training budget, trained on proportionally more tokens than that budget alone prescribes. The extra training compute is a one-time cost. The resulting model is cheaper to serve for as long as it’s deployed. That’s the logic behind training models well past the point that minimizes training compute alone: the objective was never training-compute-optimality by itself. It was total cost including serving.

RL post-training: PPO, DPO, GRPO, and their failure modes

Three RL algorithms cover most of production post-training, in lineage order:

Method Core mechanism What it removes vs. its predecessor
PPO Optimizes a policy against a separately trained reward model, using a clipped objective that permits several safe minibatch updates per batch of experience. Schulman et al., arXiv:1707.06347 (none, it’s the RLHF baseline)
DPO Solves the same reward-maximization objective in one supervised-style stage, directly on preference pairs: the optimal reward implied by a Bradley-Terry preference model has closed form in the policy itself. Rafailov et al., NeurIPS 2023 A separately trained reward model, and the RL sampling loop
GRPO Keeps on-policy RL, but drops the separate critic/value network: it samples a group of completions per prompt and uses that group’s own mean/std of reward as the baseline for each completion’s advantage. DeepSeekMath, arXiv:2402.03300 A full second model (the critic) that PPO needs to estimate value

It’s a common shorthand to say “DPO has no reward model,” but the paper’s own subtitle is the language model is secretly a reward model: DPO removes the need for a separately trained reward network, not the concept of a reward function, which becomes implicit in the policy itself. DPO, NeurIPS 2023 Each has a characteristic failure mode. PPO optimizes against a learned proxy reward rather than true human judgment, so it inherits the generic risk of any proxy-optimization setup: the policy can find outputs that score well on the reward model without being genuinely better, i.e. reward hacking. DPO never samples from the live policy during training. That leaves its quality sensitive to how well the fixed preference-pair dataset covers situations the model will actually face later. GRPO’s group-relative baseline needs variance in the group’s reward to produce a meaningful advantage signal by definition, so a group where every sampled completion scores identically (a trivially easy or a hopelessly hard prompt) yields a degenerate, near-zero learning signal for that prompt. Newer variants keep adjusting the group-baseline formula to patch instabilities like this one. The durable idea (drop the critic, keep on-policy sampling, use the group itself as the baseline) is what’s worth carrying forward, more than any one successor’s name.

Reasoning, chain-of-thought, and the honest state of the evidence

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting has the model generate intermediate reasoning steps before its final answer, which measurably helps on arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic tasks. The original paper found the effect emerges specifically at large model scale. Wei et al., arXiv:2201.11903 Test-time compute scaling generalizes this: spend more computation at answer time (longer reasoning traces, or sampling several candidates and picking the best) rather than only at training time. Learning to reason with LLMs, OpenAI Self-consistency is the clearest illustration that “longer” isn’t the mechanism: since a single long greedy trace isn’t reliable, it instead samples several independent reasoning paths and majority-votes the final answer, gaining +17.9 points on GSM8K by ensembling, not by lengthening one trace. Wang et al., arXiv:2203.11171

Misconception: “the visible chain-of-thought is a faithful, complete report of the model’s real internal computation.” Anthropic ran a controlled study planting hints in prompts and checking whether extended-thinking traces disclosed using them: on average across hint types, Claude 3.7 Sonnet mentioned the hint only 25% of the time it demonstrably used it, versus 39% for DeepSeek R1. And the two models don’t move together from there. On the “unauthorized access” hint category specifically, Claude’s faithfulness rose to 41% while R1’s fell to 19%, so this is two models diverging on different scenario types, not one model uniformly degrading. Both models sometimes fabricated plausible-sounding rationales unconnected to what actually drove the answer, and the study’s starker finding was in reward-hacking scenarios: when a model exploited a flaw to land on a rewarded-but-incorrect answer, in more than half the tested environments both models verbalized having done so less than 2% of the time. Reasoning models don’t always say what they think, Anthropic

The deeper, still-open question is whether RL-based reasoning training (RLVR, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards) teaches genuinely new reasoning ability, or mostly resamples what the base model could already do. A widely-discussed 2025 analysis found RLVR-trained models beat their base models at pass@1, but the base model wins at pass@k for large k: meaning the base model already contained correct-answer trajectories somewhere in its sampling distribution, and RL’s main effect is making the single most-likely sample better, not expanding the model’s underlying reasoning coverage. Six different RLVR algorithms tested all fell well short of the base model’s latent potential, suggesting this is a property of the approach generally, not one flawed implementation. By contrast, distillation from a stronger teacher model did introduce reasoning patterns genuinely absent from the student’s base distribution. arXiv 2504.13837

Note: a separate line of evidence points the same direction, not confirms it: Apple’s “Illusion of Thinking” study, using logic puzzles of controlled difficulty, found three regimes: plain models beat reasoning models on easy problems, reasoning models win in the middle range, and both collapse to zero accuracy at high complexity, with the reasoning model’s own effort peaking mid-difficulty and then declining even as problems got harder. Apple Machine Learning Research Treat “does RL-trained reasoning generalize” as a live, contested question: the evidence so far points toward sharpening existing capability within a bounded envelope, not open-ended new reasoning, but this isn’t the final word.

Distillation: what transfers, and what doesn’t

Knowledge distillation trains a smaller student model to reproduce a larger teacher’s soft output probabilities, not just its top-1 answer: a teacher that’s confidently right but “still considering” a related wrong answer carries more signal than a bare correct/incorrect label would. Hinton, Vinyals, Dean, arXiv:1503.02531 That graded shape of near-misses is what transfers. What doesn’t transfer is any account of why the teacher assigned those probabilities. The student sees only the numeric fingerprint of the teacher’s behavior, no accompanying explanation. Read this alongside the reasoning-generalization question above. The same analysis that found RLVR mostly resamples existing capability found distillation from a stronger teacher does introduce genuinely new reasoning patterns. Of the two levers, teacher distillation currently does more to expand what a model can do, not just how reliably it does what it already could. arXiv 2504.13837

LoRA and PEFT: when fine-tuning beats prompting or RAG

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) freezes the pretrained model’s original weights entirely and trains a much smaller pair of low-rank matrices added on top of specific weight matrices, on the hypothesis that the update a fine-tuning task needs has low intrinsic rank even when the base model doesn’t. Hu et al., arXiv:2106.09685 The original paper reports LoRA performing on par with or better than full fine-tuning on the models it tested, while cutting trainable parameters by up to 10,000x and GPU memory for fine-tuning GPT-3 175B by 3x. And because the learned low-rank update can be mathematically merged back into the base weight matrix, there’s zero added inference latency once training is done. LoRA, arXiv:2106.09685

That combination (cheap to train, free at inference) is why LoRA is the practical answer to “fine-tune, prompt, or retrieve?” for a specific job. Fine-tuning (LoRA included) reliably teaches behavior, format, and style (a consistent tone, a house JSON schema, a narrow task’s quirks) because that’s what RLHF-style post-training was shown to actually move. It’s comparatively unreliable at injecting new, precise factual knowledge, since that’s a pretraining-stage property fine-tuning mostly doesn’t touch. For getting the model to use specific, current, or proprietary facts correctly, retrieval (putting facts in the prompt at query time) is the more dependable lever. For changing how the model behaves given information it already has, fine-tuning is the right tool, and LoRA is what makes doing that on a single GPU practical at all.

Long context: the advertised-vs-effective gap

Beyond the positional-encoding choice itself, two techniques specifically address extending usable context: ALiBi biases attention scores by a distance-based penalty rather than modifying embeddings, letting a model trained short extrapolate longer at inference. Press et al., arXiv:2108.12409 YaRN instead reinterpolates an existing RoPE model’s rotation frequencies, reporting roughly 10x fewer tokens and 2.5x fewer training steps than prior extension methods to stretch a model’s effective window. YaRN, arXiv:2309.00071

But extending the technical limit and using that limit well are separate problems. Liu et al.’s “Lost in the Middle” work found a persistent U-shaped accuracy curve: models recall information at the start or end of a long context far more reliably than information buried in the middle, independent of whether the context technically fits. Lost in the Middle, arXiv:2307.03172 A harder, more recent test sharpens this: Adobe Research’s NoLiMa benchmark deliberately chooses “needle” facts sharing almost no literal wording with the test question, forcing genuine semantic inference instead of string matching. Under that test, 11 of 13 models advertising 128K+ context dropped below 50% of their own short-context score by just 32K tokens, and GPT-4o’s accuracy fell from 99.3% to 69.7% as context grew. NoLiMa defines effective length as the longest context at which a model retains at least 85% of its short-context score: by that bar, every tested model’s effective length lands well below its advertised maximum. NoLiMa, arXiv:2502.05167

Common trap: “a bigger advertised context window means the model reads that much text reliably.” Advertised and effective context are two different numbers, and the gap between them is a live, measured phenomenon, not a rare edge case.

Prefix caching: why it dominates local agent economics

Prefix caching (also called prompt caching) reuses the already-computed KV cache for a shared prefix across multiple requests (a repeated system prompt, tool definitions, or a long document reused across many follow-up turns). So the expensive attention computation for that prefix isn’t redone from scratch on every call. It changes nothing about the model’s weights. It’s a pure serving-time optimization, and it’s exact-match: the moment a shared prefix differs by even one token, everything after that point must be recomputed. This is precisely why it matters most for agents, which repeatedly re-send a large, mostly-unchanged system prompt and tool schema on every turn of a long-running task: the prefix is enormous and nearly static, exactly the shape prefix caching is built to exploit. As of July 2026, Anthropic’s own pricing puts cache reads at 10% of base input price, a 5-minute-TTL cache write at 1.25x normal input price, and a 1-hour-TTL write at 2x. Claude docs, prompt caching Locally, the same principle applies without a per-token bill attached: skipping recomputation of a fixed system prompt and tool schema every turn is the difference between an agent loop that gets slower every turn and one that doesn’t.

Agent architectures, briefly

An LLM agent interleaves generation with real actions in a loop. ReAct, the canonical formalization, has the model alternate a Thought (reasoning about what to do), an Action (a tool call), and an Observation (the real result fed back in), repeating until done. Yao et al., arXiv:2210.03629 It’s still the same next-token model as everything above, wired into a loop by code outside the model: no separate planning module. Multi-agent orchestration composes several such loops, potentially different models or personas, coordinated by a manager process. Because each agent instance has no shared state beyond the text explicitly passed between them, one agent’s ungrounded claim can be handed to another as if verified, with neither able to detect it. More agents doesn’t automatically mean more reliable, the way more human specialists usually does. For the depth this deserves (scaffolding patterns, failure modes at longer horizons, where orchestration earns its complexity), see Coding agents.

Interpretability and alignment: genuinely open

Mechanistic interpretability tries to reverse-engineer what a trained network’s components actually compute. Most individual neurons in large networks are polysemantic (they respond to a mixture of unrelated concepts), which is why Anthropic’s work on Claude 3 Sonnet trained sparse autoencoders to decompose activations into more individually-meaningful “features” instead of reading raw neurons. Human evaluators rated roughly 70% of the extracted features as genuinely interpretable. Scaling Monosemanticity, Anthropic That’s real progress, and a real gap: about 30% of extracted features weren’t judged clearly interpretable even with this method, and the technique gives a partial, feature-level account of behavior, not a complete circuit-level one, consistent with attention weights and visible chain-of-thought each separately turning out not to reliably reflect the model’s actual internal computation, above. Attention is not Explanation, arXiv:1902.10186

Alignment research aims to keep increasingly capable systems pursuing goals compatible with human interests as they exceed the competence of the humans checking their work. Anthropic’s own public framing treats the difficulty of that problem as genuinely uncertain, spanning from “existing techniques like RLHF and Constitutional AI basically suffice” through to “may not be solvable with current techniques,” as live, unresolved possibilities rather than a settled bet. Its named open directions include scalable oversight (accurate feedback on a system once it’s more capable than its evaluators), interpretability, process-oriented learning (rewarding a justified process rather than a good-looking outcome), understanding generalization (telling benign pattern-repetition apart from learned misaligned behavior), and deliberately eliciting dangerous failure modes in smaller models before they appear in more powerful ones. Anthropic’s Core Views on AI Safety Treat both “alignment is basically solved” and “alignment is provably impossible” as overclaims: the honest position is that it’s an active research program with named unsolved problems, not a finished argument.
